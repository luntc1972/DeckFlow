## Summary

The creator walks through building their $70 budget Meria, Scholar of Antiquity Commander deck. Starting from green-red commanders, they rejected the standard zero/one-cost artifact storm build as too fragile and pivoted to tapping equipments—exploiting Magic's arbitrary split between tappable artifacts and untappable enchantments. Because Meria is central and vulnerable to commander tax, they devoted 12 slots and ~$35 (half the budget) to protection: six protection equipments, four instant-speed spells, two creature-based. Equipment-synergy creatures (Goblin Gaveleer, Champion of the Flame, Valduk, Chishiro, Armory Automaton) plus reconfigure creatures fill the board, though the commander itself is the best equipment bearer via commander damage. Ramp focuses on treasure-generating equipments like Goldvein Pick to hold up interaction efficiently. Removal leans on six burn spells plus artifact-hate creatures. Combat equipments split into damage amplifiers (buffs/doublers enabling two- or one-shots) and evasion (unblockable/flying grants). The result is a fast, flexible voltron deck killing players by turn 6-7, weak to sacrifice and board wipes. Takeaways: let deckbuilding evolve fluidly, balance protection/ramp/draw/removal ratios, favor flexible multi-purpose cards, and embrace variable threat composition for replayability.

- **[02:02]** With equipments, though, you can do both—so naturally, my deckbuilding started out with six protection equipments... going into this deck where the commander is central, I decided to go all out on protection, adding four instant speed protection spells and two creature-based ones for a total of twelve slots for protection.
- **[03:39]** Starting with ramp, this deck is a good example of how stopping to think about what you're actually trying to accomplish with your ramp is a great step. I'm not trying to cast my commander on turn 2—it does nothing without artifacts in play... So what do I need ramp for? Two things—one, holding up interaction, and two, re-playing my commander.
- **[04:15]** A difficulty for faster decks is having mana to hold up interaction, since if you have a two mana spell, for the purposes of mana efficiency keeping it up at all times is effectively like getting two of your lands destroyed. This kinda sucks, and is solved beautifully by treasures, whose one-time use means that they're never wasted.
- **[07:44]** try to let deckbuilding be a fluid process. This deck actually became faster as I worked on it... it's unlikely that you'll have an idea for a deck that will work from first principles, but you can take elements of a deck that appeal to you and play around with them, and a lot of times that will lead somewhere cool.
- **[08:10]** think about the balance of the supporting card types, the basic four that come to mind being protection, ramp, draw, and removal. Figuring out the right ratios for these can be a great aid in running a deck that consistently does what it wants to do in a real game.
- **[08:26]** Tracking and thinking about these more will also have the side effect of encouraging running more flexible cards—in the case of my deck that means running artifact hate that's also an evasive flier, running ramp that's also an evasion equipment, and running a protection equipment that also buffs.
- **[09:42]** paying careful attention to the space your deck is giving to various things and optimizing for space efficiency is a great first step.
