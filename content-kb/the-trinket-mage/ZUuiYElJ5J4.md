## Summary

This Magic Mirror podcast debates the most flexible color in EDH, framed through multi-color deckbuilding rather than mono-color, since most popular commanders (Ur-Dragon, Edgar, Atraxa) run three-plus colors. Green is praised as the strongest standalone color for ramp and creature-tied removal, but the hosts argue this makes it a better primary than splash. White is called the best splash color for its removal (Generous Gift), protection (Teferi's Protection), and stax pieces. Red is contested: one host splashes it precisely because it feels weakest, while others defend it for haste (Arena of Glory), treasure fixing, damage doublers, and reach (Fling). They note red lacks enchantment removal but the primary color usually covers that. For three-color splashes, blue wins via cheap single-pip glue (counterspells, filtering, Swan Song); white's flicker (Ephemerate) also rates well. Treasure-heavy red can fix into small splashes. On mono-color fun: white for versatility, black for aesthetics, red because it's intentionally weak and puzzle-like. They warn against trusting deck-ranking tools (Commander Salt) and ChatGPT for deckbuilding, since LLMs regurgitate poor forum advice and don't read cards; real friend-group discussion is better.

## Key Clips

- When people say what's the strongest color, they think mono-color, but in reality the most popular commanders are Ur-Dragon (five color), Edgar (three), Atraxa (four), Yistola (three), with Krenko the odd mono one out. Outside Giada and Krenko there isn't another mono-colored commander in the top 30. More often than not you are playing a multi-color deck, so what the strongest color is changes based on its flexibility as a splash or main color.
- The most flexible color in terms of power level is green. It gives land ramp the other colors don't have, and green can always do anything as long as it vaguely involves a creature ΓÇö enchantment removal, artifact removal, creature removal, plus big draw like Rishkar's Expertise (draw when a creature enters or equal to a creature's power). Its ceiling isn't as high but it's very versatile.
- White is the best splash color. A little splash of white makes any deck stronger because white has such good removal, slow-down tools, and protection ΓÇö real protection spells instead of just lightning greaves. Generous Gift hits anything; Teferi's Protection is the best protection spell. White's stax cards also make it powerful even beyond MLD.
- Counterpoint for red as a splash: when splashing a color you're not splashing just for removal, you're splashing for things that make your primary game plan better ΓÇö haste enablers on lands (Arena of Glory), damage doublers/amplifiers, filtering and looting, treasures, and reach (Fling, burn). Your primary color already covers enchantment removal, so red's lack of it doesn't matter as a splash.
- Red may be the best small/tertiary splash because random red cards are some of the most powerful in the game ΓÇö Deflecting Swat, Jeska's Will, Underworld Breach as combo extenders. Treasures encourage running more red with small splashes of other colors: a single-pip Professional Face-Breaker or Storm-Kiln Artist makes treasures that fix for your off-colors. Big Score (single red, discard a card, draw two, make two treasures) is broken in storm decks for filtering since most rituals make red mana.
- Blue is the easiest third color to splash ΓÇö so many single-pip glue cards like cheap counterspells, Swan Song, An Offer You Can't Refuse, and strong filtering (Brainstorm-style 'look at top five, take two'). You can run a handful of filtering and counters to protect a red-white Jeskai game plan without blue being a main part of the deck.
- On Overburden (1U enchantment: when a player puts a non-token creature onto the battlefield, that player returns a land they control to hand) ΓÇö it's bracket-three borderline, run it but rule-zero it. At two mana it walls many players. It's a card that could be a game changer but isn't, only because nobody has seen it in years; same with Bottomless Pit.
- Don't use ChatGPT to refine decks ΓÇö LLMs regurgitate crap forum posts written by people who also can't build good mana bases, and they don't actually read the cards, just recognize card names. A small purpose-built model beat pro poker players where no LLM could; a general model isn't specific enough to Magic. Best deck advice comes from your friend-group Discord or local game store, because they know your playgroup's power level and what you like.
