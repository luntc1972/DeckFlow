## Summary

Salubrious Snail's Alex critiques "Smol Bean Syndrome" (SBS): players who verbally downplay their deck or conceal their game state to discourage attacks and lengthen games. Some SBS is genuine—inconsistent decks loaded with explosive cards but lacking connective tissue have real whiff moments. The deckbuilding fix is more filtering, small-scale draw, low-cost interaction, and small ramp spells to smooth the power curve so the deck reaches its payoffs more reliably. Alex contrasts this with "transparent" decks like his Glissa battlecruiser (ramp, draw, removal, big creatures—threat visible on board), Meria Voltron (problem sits on the board), and a $25 Rakdos discard deck. Transparency lets opponents gauge your position from boardstate, hand size, and land count. He advocates "fight-the-table ideology": pose a clear, consistent threat and be openly straightforward about how the deck wins. This works best in mixed-power games or when one player jumps ahead. Counterintuitively, visible consistent threats often draw less hate—opponents calculate "it'll probably be fine"—while opaque, explosive, or unknown plays invite fear and attacks. Ramp decks exemplify transparency dodging removal. Combo decks resist this approach because they rely on hidden cards in hand. He suggests interrogating whether each deck could be built and played transparently.

## Key Clips

- I like to call this style of table engagement SBS—"Smol Bean Syndrome"—when a player goes out of their way to paint their deck as being in a bad spot and deserving of nothing but mercy. I'm not a big fan of most forms of Smol Bean Syndrome due to their tendency to discourage attacks and artificially lengthen games.
- A deck that dedicates too much space to the synergistic and the explosive will have some big turns and occasionally massive wins, but also a lot of moments where it withers to a single removal spell. These moments could be made less frequent by running more of that connective tissue—more filtering, more small-scale draw, and more small creatures and low-cost interaction cards to protect themself.
- My Glissa deck consists of ramp, draw spells, removal, and big creatures. No bursty wins, no surprises, and no weird tech pieces. It's a very consistent deck, and it's also what I'll call a transparent deck—you can always approximate how well it's doing by looking at the boardstate, cards in hand, and land count. I call it Midrange-Crusher 5000 for a reason.
- Fight-the-table ideology: give your opponents the information they need to know and be open about the general shape of what your deck is doing, specifically how it wins and what cards you're playing are doing there, and suddenly you've got a dynamic game where your opponents are strategizing about how to keep you in check while managing their own individual plans. Originally coined for mixed power level games.
- Step one of fight-the-table ideology is that you should pose some sort of clear threat to the table with a good degree of consistency—early damage, big midgame beats, boardwipes, stax. The point is that what you're doing needs to be visible. If the threat is visible, people can make their own educated calculations. You can accomplish this step on a shockingly low budget.
- Step two is to be open about the threat your deck poses. Hardcore ramp decks work well because everybody knows they're scary at a lot of mana, but there's no singular land drop that signals the deck being a problem. It evokes more of a gentle gradient of concern rather than the black and white concern of a random spooky permanent, and this can lead to the deck flying under the radar.
- Decks with combos are generally more difficult to play transparently, as they're naturally less transparent due to the level of importance they place on cards in hand, which is by definition a hidden zone. A good exercise for every deck is thinking about what it would have to do to become a good candidate for this. If your deck is sometimes a slow midrange deck and other times an explosive combo list, that might be something to interrogate.
