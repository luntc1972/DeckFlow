## Summary

This video explains how to use card categories effectively when building Commander/EDH decks. Categories serve two main purposes: capturing the core tasks cards perform (lands, ramp, draw, protection, interaction) so you can see a deck's shape, and tracking synergy packages via paired setup and payoff categories to check whether a theme is well supported. Suggested core ranges: 35-40 lands, 15-20 removal, 13-18 draw, 7-12 ramp, 3-8 protection. When building a ramp category, ask why you're ramping—hitting cards a turn early favors curve-specific and ritual options, while raw mana volume favors bigger pieces. Define interaction by function along a proactive-to-reactive spectrum (protection, counterspells, removal, defense) and split or combine buckets based on whether pieces serve distinct roles. The speaker warns against 'theme' or 'plan' categories because most theme cards already fill core roles, and such buckets create false justifications for weak cards. Avoid grouping superficially similar but functionally opposite cards (Thalia vs. Ghostly Prison) and avoid overcategorizing cards whose tracked trait doesn't affect decisions. Deckbuilder search/highlight tools (Archidekt, Moxfield) replace some categories and help evaluate maybeboard packages. Ultimately, whatever helps you conceptualize your deck is correct.

## Key Clips

- Categories capture the core tasks that the cards in your deck are performing. I always start out with categories of card that most decks will want at least some of—principally lands, ramp, draw, protection, and interaction. These categories are useful because all on their own they can start to paint a picture of the shape of what a deck is doing.
- If somebody asked me for healthy starting ranges for their core categories for a bog-standard EDH deck, I'd say something like 35-40 lands, 15-20 pieces of removal-equivalent, 13-18 pieces of draw, 7-12 pieces of ramp, and 3-8 pieces of protection. If you go on the higher end of each of those values, that adds up to 98 deck slots—you got a whole one leftover to work with.
- When constructing your ramp category, you should be asking yourself "why am I ramping?" If your ramp is geared to hit specific cards a turn early, you'll want to be specific with the curve of your mana, and it might even be worth including ritual-style cards. By contrast, if your ramp is generally geared toward increasing the volume of your mana without specific timing goals, less card-efficient options like smaller ramp and ritual-style cards won't make as much sense.
- One categorical starting point I've used before is having two slightly-fuzzy buckets—protection + counterspells, and removal + defense. It makes sense if you think about all interaction in terms of how proactive vs reactive it is. Protection is very proactive, counterspells are similar but with more flexibility to hit opposing threats, removal is more reactive, and defensive cards are the most reactive—the final line of defense in an EDH game.
- The second major thing categories do is allowing people to keep track of synergies. For each synergy package I'll include two categories—setup, and payoff. The utility is allowing you to keep an eye on how well supported a specific theme is. If you don't maintain a setup category, you might not even clock a problem and might presume that these cards not working out in games is due to bad luck.
- A side benefit of categorizing a deck—putting cards into categories requires a deckbuilder to think about why a card is there, and any cards that didn't end up in many categories immediately become suspect and deserving of additional scrutiny. This is one of the reasons I'm wary of "plan" or "theme" categories—they give an easy place to toss cards that might otherwise seem out of place or underjustified.
- Avoid putting cards together that are similar on a superficial level but very different in function. Thalia, Guardian of Thraben and Ghostly Prison both impose taxes, but the game actions they tax are basically polar opposites—Thalia's good for slowing more technical decks, whereas Ghostly Prison makes attacking more costly but does nothing to slow anything outside combat. The former is proactive, the latter purely reactive, and merging them into one category is a recipe for confusion.
- The other thing to avoid is overcategorizing—creating a category to track a type of card that isn't actually useful to track. The bigger issue is when categories are used to justify the presence of cards that really might not belong. With a lifegain category I've constructed a justification for a card out of thin air, and might choose to keep this unnecessary card in my deck as a result.
