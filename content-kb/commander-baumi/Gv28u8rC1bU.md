## Summary

This video argues that effective Commander deck building is primarily a process of exclusion: with ~30,000 cards available, filling 100 slots is trivial, so the skill lies in identifying which cards to cut. The creator criticizes over-categorizingΓÇögiving every card its own granular tagΓÇöbecause it makes every card seem purposeful and hides which cards don't actually fit the deck's game plan (illustrated by a self-mill tag justifying useless cards). Instead, he sorts cards into three broad, deliberately loose categories present in every deck: card advantage (~20-30 pieces, including weak draw like occasional scry), mana (~equal number; for aggro this can be taxing opponents via Rule of Law rather than ramp), and interaction (~10, sometimes up to 20 for defensive decks). Two minor categories follow: finishers (5-8 cards that push the game toward a win) and theme-only (kept minimal, only for cards fitting no other category). Broad definitions are adjusted per deck (self-mill can count as card advantage). He targets 80-90% thematic cards that still function, occasionally splitting a category (e.g., mana into land enchantments/untappers) when distribution demands it. Curve and early/late game plans still matter alongside categories.

## Key Clips

- For a deck to function well, every card within it should serve a purpose. This is true for all brackets, for all power levels. Cards that don't support the ideas of the deck will lead to worse game experiences as they're essentially dead draws, which can sometimes feel like losing an entire turn.
- In practice, many decks are categorized so intricately that it invalidates the point of the exercise. If every single card is categorized, then everything seems useful, even when it's in a category that doesn't really belong in the deck.
- It's an inverted process because ideally, we create categories first and then find cards that fit into them rather than choosing cards and then making up descriptions for them.
- Commander deck building is really primarily a process of exclusion. Filling up 100 cards is trivially easy. There are like 30,000 to choose from. So we need to focus on identifying which cards we don't want to play.
- I start out with three major categories that I then continuously sort cards into: card advantage, mana, and interaction. These are incredibly broad descriptors. Anything that can give us access to additional cards is card advantage; anything that creates mana is mana; counter spells, targeted removal, protection, sweepers, graveyard hate, all of that is interaction.
- I generally shoot for at least 20 pieces of card advantage, an equal number of mana, and 10ish pieces of interaction. Ideally, there's a good amount of overlap between the different functions as well. There is no universal distribution here.
- In addition to these three major categories, I like having two minor ones: finishers and theme only cards. I have found that 5 to 8 is a good number of finishers. Theme only is a catch all category that we want to keep as small as possible ΓÇö the more theme only cards we play, the less structurally sound our deck becomes.
- By being so broad in our definitions, we can include stuff that only occasionally draws a card as card advantage. But if we do that, we then also need to include a much higher number of those cards ΓÇö like 30 pieces of card advantage ΓÇö so that we can play the bad things. If we have that high quantity, then we can actually play really anything we want.
