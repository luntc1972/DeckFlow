## Summary

The creator analyzes deck-review data showing more decks needed ramp (9) than card draw (6), then builds a framework for deciding ramp counts. Ramp serves two distinct functions: curving out (deploying cards on an ideal timeline, e.g., hitting a 4-mana commander on turn 4) and increasing total mana over the game. For curving out, match ramp mana values to your non-ramp curve, weigh mana dorks (efficient but boardwipe-fragile) against 3-mana rocks (synergy/upside), and account for taplands. Quantify needs with a hypergeometric calculator or benchmarks: 18 copies if always wanted opening hand, 15 every game, 12 most games, 9 frequent, 6 sometimes, 3 rarely. Total-mana ramp matters for expensive commanders, command tax, and slow draw-heavy decks; prefer permanent ramp (lands, then rocks). New decks should start high on draw and ramp. A deck wants little ramp when it has cheap proactive plays (e.g., a low-curve equipment or discard deck) and no late-game mana sink. Citing Karsten and Black, the creator argues many decks should favor more lands over ramp. Test: if you repeatedly miss land drops while holding ramp, swap ramp for lands.

## Key Clips

- Ramp spells don't fulfill one single task. Rather, they perform two separate functions—curving out, and increasing total mana quantity.
- What I mean by curving out is deploying your cards in an order and on a timeline that lets your deck best perform its gameplan in the first five turns of the game. Most Pantlaza players would prefer to get him out on turn 4, since Dinosaurs played after him generate extra value.
- Ramping on turn 1 is theoretically the most efficient from a mana-spent standpoint, but basically all 1 mana ramp will be in the form of mana dorks, which could amplify the weakness to boardwipes. Meanwhile, 3 mana ramp is less mana efficient but likely to have upsides beyond just boosting us up 1 mana, and might contain more cards that synergize with the rest of our gameplan.
- If increased maximum mana is the role of ramp you're leaning into, you're going to be best-served to prioritize more permanent forms of ramp—preferably land ramp, mana rocks in a pinch, and mana dorks at your own caution. An Elvish Mystic will be great for a buttery-smooth curveout, but an EDH game is a hostile climate for a squishy guy like that in the long term.
- Benchmarks for a card role: 18 cards if you always want it in your opening hand and are okay with multiples; 15 if it's a tool you want every game with a bit of wiggle room; 12 for most games but some have alternate options; 9 for a frequent part of curve-out but not core; 6 for a thing you want sometimes; 3 for a thing you don't want to draw early very often.
- My Meria voltron deck is packed with 1 and 2 mana equipment, so by the standard of curveout it wouldn't want any ramp at all. But I still run a moderate quantity, because this deck always wants to be able to pay commander tax. So this is a deck that doesn't want ramp for curveout reasons, but does want it for total mana reasons.
- Frank Karsten and Sam Black have each weighed in, and both concluded through simulations that the average EDH deck would be served well to focus more on land count and less on quantity of ramp. A lot of ramp and a low land count is frequently a recipe for inefficiency.
- Here's a basic test: whenever you miss a land drop with a piece of ramp in hand, or a piece of ramp that was recently played, make note of that. If that is happening every game or even multiple times per game, that's an indication that you probably need to swap some ramp spells for some lands.
