## Summary

This video uses Sol Ring as a launching point for a broader argument about EDH deckbuilding. The host recaps Sol Ring's history—Reserved List, the 2011 Commander precons—and argues it won't and shouldn't be banned, though he dislikes running it in lower-power decks. The core point: cheap, powerful staples like Sol Ring, Counterspell, Swords to Plowshares, and Path to Exile warp how players evaluate cards. Sorting by EDHRec rank, players grab the few strongest, cheapest options and under-fill supporting categories (ramp, draw, removal, counters) that actually want 8–10 cards. This creates 'bimodal' decks that either explode when key pieces connect or fall flat. He warns that free cards you already own (e.g., Sword of Forge and Frontier) can warp a deck's power level and shift how opponents perceive it. Examples include graveyard decks needing resilience against hate, cheap infinite combos changing game dynamics, and his brother's Jodah deck struggling at higher tables. Key advice: decide how many of each supporting effect a deck needs before browsing; the strongest card isn't always the best fit; and add cards based on the kind of games you want to play, even when they're free.

## Key Clips

- Sol Ring was first printed in 1993 in Alpha and is one of the most powerful mana artifacts in the game, banned or restricted in most constructed formats for basically its entire existence. It was printed in the first commander set in 2011, included in all five preconstructed decks.
- Cheap, powerful cards warp the way players look at other cards. Rather than building a framework first—'this deck wants eight ramp spells, ten draw spells, twelve removal spells'—most people see their options for ramp, draw, and removal and just start adding cards, letting the specific cards available dictate the final quantities.
- If you order counterspells by EDHRec rank, you first see Counterspell at a dollar, then options with downsides or high prices. It's easy to imagine a player running Counterspell plus a couple others and stopping, when most blue decks actually want enough lowercase-c counterspells that they'd benefit from running cards a lot worse than Counterspell.
- A deck running too much synergistic stuff and not enough general stuff risks a bimodal pickle—a state where it either falls on its face after a key piece gets removed, or goes on a mach 10 march toward victory. Card quality gets a lot less consistent at lower budgets.
- You should add cards under consideration of how they'll impact the games you play, even if you own them and they're free to add. The author cut Sword of Forge and Frontier from a Meria equipments deck because it had such a warping effect that it felt like a totally different deck and shifted how opponents perceived its power level.
- Graveyard decks must make a decision: accept the need to be resilient against heavy graveyard hate (Tormod's Crypt, Rest in Peace, Bojuka Bog), usually by winning quickly or loading up on counters, or play at a power level where these cards are less common. Mid-power graveyard decks sit in an awkward spot—stronger synergies crush unprepared decks but still get dunked on by hate.
- Adding an infinite combo like Niv-Mizzet and Curiosity changes the whole dynamic of the games once people know it's there. If you're purely looking to increase power level it's fine logic, but it shifts how opponents approach you—an aggro player will start hitting the combo player early.
- A deck built with the tools it needs at a slightly lower power level performs with much more consistency than a deck that only picks the strongest 3-4 cards for categories where it really wants 8 or 10. Commander is theoretically self-balancing, but inconsistently powerful decks become harder for others to gauge, leading them to either hit your deck or pick a more powerful deck to begin with.
