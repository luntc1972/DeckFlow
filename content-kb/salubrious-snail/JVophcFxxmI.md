## Key Clips

- A consistent deck is one that consistently does something, and whose things it does don't vary all that much in power level. This often means decks with multiple avenues of play whose construction is good enough that the different options all feel valuable.
- From a table politics standpoint, people will remember the most powerful moments of the deckΓÇöyour spooky pop-off win you can only pull off 10% of the time will be remembered alongside a more consistent deck's quite solid gameplan it pulls off 75% of the time, and this will make your deck seem disproportionately scary.
- For starters, one of the easier forms of consistency to nail is what I like to call "rollout consistency". This type of consistency basically asks the question, "do I do what I want to do on the first several turns of the game?" The best way of doing this is solo playtesting a deck a whole bunch.
- A simple benchmark you can reference is that you need 10 of a card in a deck to have it show up in a given hand more than half the time. If I have 10 ramp spells in the deck there's a 63% chance of having one on turn 2, if there's 15 in deck I have a 79% chance, if there's 20 I have an 88% chance.
- A good first step toward reducing filler is to categorize your deck. Four supporting categories I usually have are Ramp, Draw, Removal, and Protection. Separating these out and making sure you have enough of each is a good first step of making sure that you're spending slots on things that will actually make your deck better.
- For removal I'd recommend starting between 8 and 14, and adjust from there keeping in mind that slower decks and lower-velocity decks will usually want more removal than faster decks and higher-velocity decks will. Protection is the most variable of allΓÇöI run 0 in a couple scrappy decks and 12 in the Meria deck.
- If a card doesn't fit into one of your key categories, you should be asking yourself what its purpose is, and the answer should be simple and straightforward. If the answer involves scratching your head and vaguely gesturing at a couple other cards, that's a card that deserves a more robust evaluation.
- The best way to allow a deck to have easy access to situational cards without destroying the deck's general consistency is tutors. A natural place to end up is often what's called a "toolbox" deckΓÇöa deck with a number of conditional tutors that can each grab cards useful in several different situations. A couple quintessential toolbox cards are Birthing Pod and Yisan, the Wanderer Bard.
