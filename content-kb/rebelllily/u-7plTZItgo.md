## Summary

This video teaches Commander deck engine design: building a repeatable system that converts one resource into another at scale, rather than just piling on theme-matching cards. An engine has inputs (enablers) and outputs (payoffs). Enablers are the backbone and should be cheap in mana value, self-sufficient (work alone without needing other cards), and consistent/repeatable (upkeep triggers, activated abilities, or common triggered effects). Avoid expensive enablers like Wolverine Riders or multipliers like Rox Faithmender in enabler slots; reserve the top of the curve for payoffs. Payoffs convert the resource into game-ending threats (Archangel of Thune, Vito, Angelic Accord), not just more card draw (Dawn of Hope, Well of Lost Springs maintain momentum but don't close games). Play fewer payoffs than enablers since payoffs are dead without enablers. Recommended ratio: at least 12 inputs and 6 outputs, leaving 3 theme slots for synergy multipliers (Parallel Lives effects). The commander itself counts as an input or output and shifts the ratioΓÇöThrasios is a payoff dump, Aluren-style eminence is a cheap enabler. Build by starting with your win-condition payoffs and working backwards to choose supporting enablers, then goldfish the deck.

## Key Clips

- An engine is a repeatable system that converts one resource into another one at scale. Kind of like a factory. The process of the conversion between these two things requires an input and an output, or more commonly known as the enabler versus the payoff.
- The key to picking the right enablers: you want them cheap in mana value, because if your entire engine depends on this resource you don't want to wait till later in the game to get it going. You want them self-sufficient ΓÇö work by themselves, your workhorse cards. And you want them consistent ΓÇö repeatable in how they generate the resource, like an upkeep trigger, an activated ability, or a triggered ability off a common effect.
- The most common mistake I see is spotting the obvious theme for a commander and making the deck a pile of cards that fit the theme without picking the right ones to create a fluid engine. Just because you fill a deck with stuff that you can synergize and trigger off your commander, doesn't mean it's always going to work.
- A bad enabler is over-indexing on big cool cards like Rox Faithmender, a multiplier to double life gain, or Sunscorch Regent that comes in way too late, or Zhur-Taa Ancient that needs an attack trigger which isn't guaranteed. The inputs are your setup cards and the most important thing is they need to be cheap. At that top end of the mana curve, we're saving those spots for our payoffs.
- Outputs should focus more on threats or game enders, like Archangel of Thune, Vito, Thorn of the Dusk Rose, or Angelic Accord. The big problem with cards like Dawn of Hope and Well of Lost Springs is they convert life gain into card draw but don't close the game. The emphasis we want with payoffs is that they actually pay off to winning the game.
- Using the cube theory model: I always start with at least eight inputs and eight outputs because that's easier to remember. But it really should be at least 12 inputs and six outputs. We should play fewer payoffs than enablers, as most payoffs are generally useless until there is an enabler to support them.
- With 12 inputs you have a 60.7% chance of opening with one or more in your hand and 20% of opening two or more. By turn four with 12 inputs you have a 77.8% chance to see at least one input and 39.7% to see at least two. With six outputs you have a 51.6% chance to see at least one by turn four if you only draw one card per turn.
- Your commander dictates the resource type and also controls the count of inputs or outputs because it can fit into either category. If your commander is an output like Thrasios, you don't need that many more payoffs. When designing the engine, start with some of the payoffs you want to win with and work in reverse so you can pick the right backbone to support it.
