## Summary

This video argues the cycling mechanic creates rich strategic gameplay by making cycling cards and mana into resources players must carefully manageΓÇödeciding whether to cycle for value, hold cards as lifelines to protect creatures or disrupt opponents, or gamble on end-step draws. Astral Slide is presented as the classic core of cycling decks, rewarding probabilistic, resource-tension play. The creator criticizes Gavi, Nest Warden as a 'designer commander' that gives one free cycle per turn, removing the resource-management dimension and pushing players toward generic value-pile midrange decks. Gavi's only quirk is incentivizing high-cost cycling cards like Decree of Annihilation, which the creator finds unfun. Eligeth is cited as another mechanic-flattening designer commander. As an alternative, the creator built a $100 Esper Sharuum, Hand of Cyrus deck around Astral Slide and Overbooked Cemetery, using Magister Sphinx as a wincon, big flying artifact creatures for beatdown/reanimation, enchantment tutors (Idyllic Tutor, Zur) and wizardcycling for a toolbox. Key lesson: build ground-up from a mechanic and key cards, pick the commander afterward, and iterate the deck across four versionsΓÇöbrain, draft, solo-playtest, and post-multiplayer.

## Key Clips

- The cycling mechanic: you can pay an alternate cost on a card and discard it in order to draw a card. As an alternate cost ability it adds a lot of flexibility to cards, which leads to interesting decisionmaking. Every cycling card a player draws prompts the question 'is this card better than a different card plus a small mana commitment?' This is nice in aggro when running out of gas might be a concern, and even more useful in a control deck, where that mana cost can be spent on the end of an opponent's turn to dig for answers.
- Astral Slide decks revolve heavily around strategic resource management. Number of cycling cards in hand constantly dictates the strategic direction of the deckΓÇöwhen you have more, you can get bolder and set up for more daring turns, using the slide triggers more liberally as blocker elimination or to net small value off ETBs. By contrast, when you have fewer cycling cards in hand, each one is pure gold. You always want to save them for protecting a critical creature or disrupting a dangerous play from an opponent.
- Gavi, Nest Warden is a 5 mana 2/5 who makes the first cycle each turn free, and creates a cat when you draw your second card each turn. Free cycles on each turn removes an entire dimension of the cycling mechanic, and highly encourages a stable quantity of cyclingΓÇöone per turn. This ability removes the status of cycling cards as an important resource to be saved and used carefully, and leaves players with a lot of extra mana they didn't have to plan outΓÇöspent on permanents that generate value when cards get cycled.
- Wizards has a habit of printing what I'll call 'designer commanders'ΓÇöcommanders that take a particular mechanic and fully dedicate themselves to it, dictating a very specific and often very boring type of deck. A popular typographic format is one with two lines of text: 'whenever X happens, Y happens' and 'whenever enough Ys happen, a Z happens'. This is a template that builds its own deckΓÇöyou run cards that do X and Y, and maybe some Z payoff.
- The spite deck: started with Astral Slide and Overbooked Cemetery, added blue for cycling and instant-speed shenanigans, and settled on Sharuum as commander in Esper colors. Ground rules: no Sculpting Steel type combos, $100 budget. Magister Sphinx combined with Sharuum can one-shot a player. Sharuum's nature as a reanimation tool means a beatdown suite of big flying artifact creatures is a good bet at hundred-dollar-cycling-deck power levels.
- Zur's wizard tag is oddly important, as WotC printed two Wizardcycling cardsΓÇöbasically instant speed wizard tutors that count as a cycle. Wizardcycling grabs a tutor for slide, a tutor for top-end creatures, a recursion spell for slides, and a stifle effect that's also an extra cycle for big turns. The strength is flexible, ground-up deck construction: by starting with a mechanic and a couple key cards and selecting a commander afterward, the deck has a foundation that includes the commander but doesn't wholly consist of the commander.
- Deckbuilding takeaway: figure out what the deck wants to do, and turn that into a somewhat focused plan. Accept that you won't nail the first version. There are four key versions of any single deck: the first principles version in your brain, the draft version after you hammer out a 100 card list, the streamlined version after you solo playtest, and the 'final' version after you play it against other people. Each step solves a different set of problems.
