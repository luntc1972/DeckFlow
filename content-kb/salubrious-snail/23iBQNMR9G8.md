## Summary

The video argues that perceived power imbalances at an EDH table usually stem not from card power levels but from two fixable deckbuilding gaps: too little interaction and too much variance. Without enough removal and counterspells, no player can trade one-for-one to check whoever pulls ahead, so even small advantages compound. Variance grows when decks load up on payoff cardsΓÇöamplification cards (doublers that multiply tokens, mana, counters) and conversion cards (that turn one resource into another)ΓÇöwithout enough building blocks to fuel them, producing all-or-nothing games. The creator illustrates with a Zaxara deck whose doublers and lone hydra collapsed to a single removal spell. As a contrast, he describes a 2019ΓÇô2022 playgroup of ten tuned $30 decks built in a near-competitive meta, prizing consistency, resiliency, and grind. His Dimir rogues list runs six counterspells, six removal, two modal, three stax, plus 25 evasive creatures whose value doesn't depend on being ahead; a Rakdos discard deck runs heavy removal and 25 discard effects. These interaction-dense, low-setup decks fit varied tables and stay relevant when behind. The takeaway: reduce swinginess by adding interaction and cutting unnecessary or hard-to-fulfill payoffs.

## Key Clips

- Power level differences within a table can be a tricky thing to navigate... But, do you wanna know a secret? In most cases like this, the relative power levels of the decks aren't even the main issue. Decks with varied power levels can work fine in the same pod, and games can avoid this "blowout" issue, but this requires a couple things to be true, and the first one of these things is very simple: decks should run interaction.
- A lot of decks throw in some mid-single-digits number of generic removal spells and/or counter spells, and then call it good... Then, when a pod of these decks show up to a table, before too long a player will naturally start to get ahead, and their opponents won't have a lot of recourse for it. This amplifies power differences, because if a deck is even a little bit better at what it does... there will be nothing to prevent that advantage from piling up at an interaction-less table.
- A compounding factor for this sort of dynamic is when decks have high variance. I think the best way to quantify this is to think about the difference between the deck's 75th percentile situation... and the deck's 25th percentile situation... a lot of casual EDH decks I've seen were built putting so much emphasis on the ideal scenario, the big pop off factor, that as much as 25% or 30% or even 40% of the time they fail to perform meaningful game actions. This comes down to one thing above all othersΓÇöputting in too many payoff cards and not enough building blocks to get there.
- I'd categorize payoff cards into two types. One is amplification cardsΓÇöcards that convert your tokens into more tokens, your counters into more counters... if you run too many of these and too few things to be amplified, your game plan will be fragile. A while back I had a series of games with a Zaxara player who would play multiple counter-doublers and mana-doublers... followed by a single premium hydra... a single removal spell was played, the Zaxara player had no countermagic, and just like that they and their pile of synergy permanents were rendered a total non-threat.
- The other main type of payoff card is what I'll call conversion cardsΓÇöones that give you one type of resource as a result of having or spending a different one... running too many of these resource conversion cards will have problematic results for deck consistencyΓÇöif your deck's core building blocks already require you to be doing well in some capacity, you're limiting your ability to get your gameplan going in the first place.
- From 2019 to 2022, my playgroup... played exclusively with a pool of ten $30 EDH decks... In this environment, decks are built with a high degree of consistency and a lot of interaction, because these factors lead decks to win more games... Take a Dimir rogues list I builtΓÇöit runs six counterspells, six removal spells, two modal cards that can counter or remove, and three stax cards... even something like Bident of Thassa generates acceptable amounts of value with a single evasive creature, of which the deck is running 25.
- A Rakdos discard deck from the same pool is similarly heavy on interactionΓÇöit's running ten cards plus the commander for removal, as well as 25 cards that cause opponents to discard cards. The deck's cards also work well on their own as a matter of necessityΓÇöwhen a deck's gameplan involves forcing the table into a hellbent state, it's not gonna play a bunch of stuff that requires lots of setup.
- If you're finding your games too frequently feeling like a roll of the dice... just know that swinginess is a malleable feature. You can move along this spectrum of gameplay, and reducing the swinginess of your games can be as simple as following the two deckbuilding tips I've been mentioning in this videoΓÇörunning more interaction, and improving a deck's consistency and resiliency by removing payoff cards that are unnecessary or difficult-to-fulfill.
