## Summary

This video discusses ways to orient an EDH (Commander) deck relative to its commander, highlighting one underrated approach. The conventional method picks a thematically matching commander and synergy cards (e.g., Krenko leading a goblins deck), but here the commander and deck do the same thing. A stronger option is having the commander do something tangential to the deck. The speaker's favorite approach is building a midrange deck with a deliberate weakness that the commander solvesΓÇöthe commander does the opposite of what the deck does. Two examples: a Glissa Sunslayer deck where Glissa serves as a reliable blocker and draw engine, freeing the deck to skip early creatures and card draw and instead load up on ramp, removal, and big cards (a battlecruiser deck). And a Liesa deck built as hatebears rather than the usual lifegain shell; the hatebears chip damage, prevent damage, and hinder opponents while Liesa, castable for 5 mana any game, cuts through stalled boards. The formula: let the commander cover one element so the deck can devote its slots to what it does best, solving a known weakness ahead of time rather than agonizing over deck slots. Midrange decks can be fun built this way.

## Key Clips

- A pretty conventional way of constructing a commander deck: you find a commander that looks cool, you find cards that synergize well with said commander, and then you fill in the gaps with the other thing the deck needs to succeed.
- You're building a 99 card singleton deck, something with a naturally high degree of variability, but you have an ace up your sleeve: a card that you will always have, every single game.
- A lot of strong decks will opt to have the commander do something tangential to the rest of the deck, tying in with some key cards but broadly offering something different in nature than the rest of the deck. However, my favorite form of this relationship is where the commander does the exact opposite of the thing that the rest of the deck is trying to do.
- Basically you pick out a commander, and then you build a midrange-y deck with a glaring weakness solved by that commander.
- With Glissa SunslayerΓÇöan absurdly effective blocker, a draw engineΓÇöI instead opted to build a hardcore battlecruiser deck. The commander's flexibility and daunting presence means that I can fully forget about early game creatures and not worry as much about draw, instead jamming my deck to the brim with ramp, removal, and massive cards.
- Most people put Liesa in a lifegain deck, but instead I built a hatebears deck. By design, it's a deck with roughly a Liesa-shaped hole, and I'm able to play Liesa for 5 mana anytime I want in any game I want. The cards chip in damage, prevent damage, and hinder opponents, then Liesa cuts through the resulting board.
- Running a commander that covers a particular element of the deck allows a player to build a deck that puts that element on the backburner, instead focusing all its effort on the things that it knows it can succeed at. This way, you can effectively just solve the problem you know your deck will have ahead of time instead of agonizing over how many deck slots to devote to it.
- Midrange is often something that people look down on for being boring or bland, but with some creative usage of the rules of the format you can build a really fun deck that exists within the midrange realm, and I think more people should try doing that.
