## Summary

This video analyzes why cEDH decks share so many cards. Comparing the top 10 EDH Top 16 decks, the creator found 20 cards run by all ten (nine lands, five mana rocks, four counterspells, two draw enchantments), plus color-specific staples like Dockside Extortionist and Demonic Tutor. The dominant wincons are Thassa's Oracle–Demonic Consultation (7 decks) and Underworld Breach–Brain Freeze–Lion's Eye Diamond (6 decks). Overlap stems from the format demanding maximum speed, flexibility, and efficiency; singleton 100-card decks force running many differently-named copies of each effect type (12+ counterspells vs. ~9 in Modern). Budgetless manabases push decks toward 4-5 colors, producing 'good stuff' piles differentiated only by subtle tutor targets. The format's culture solves the rule-0 and proxy debates: expectations align at maximum power, reducing salt. Off-meta builds (e.g., the Trinket Mage's mono-blue Jace) can still win via matchup unfamiliarity and meta-tuned interaction. Deck predictability enables tech like Praetor's Grasp, Mnemonic Betrayal, and Ragavan that exploit shared cards. Key casual takeaway: apply cEDH's card-quality filter—prioritize flexible, efficient cards that minimize dead draws—while recognizing that aligning power levels and expectations before a game is what makes casual EDH fun.

## Key Clips

- I looked up the top 10 cEDH decks and a popular decklist for each and compared the lists to find out how many cards they shared. All told, there were 20 cards shared by all ten decks, with nine of these being lands, five being mana rocks, four being counterspells, and two being card draw enchantments.
- Most interesting of all were the wincons, with the two dominant ones being Thassa's Oracle-Demonic Consultation, run in 7 of the 10 decks, and Underworld Breach-Brain Freeze-Lion's Eye Diamond, run in 6 of the 10 decks.
- EDH is different from Modern or Legacy in that it's 100 cards and singleton. This means that, for a given type of card, you need to play a lot more different copies of that type of card than in a 60 card format. A similarly interactive cEDH deck will often end up running 12 or more differently-named counterspells.
- Draw usually ends up being things that abuse either the multiplayer aspect of EDH or the 40 life aspect—Rhystic Study, Mystic Remora, and Esper Sentinel are ubiquitous, and Ad Nauseam is so powerful that it blurs the line between draw spell and one-card wincon.
- cEDH is an essentially budgetless format, which allows for very good manabases at very little gameplay expense to the player, barring nonbasic hate. This, paired with the singleton nature of the format reducing the number of efficient options in each color, means that decks will generally want as many colors as possible.
- I've exclusively been playing Jace Vryn's Prodigy at tournaments since 2016. My list for example does not run thassa's oracle so I am able to win through hate like hushbringer or torpor orb that people commonly play against oracle decks. So don't be afraid to try some lower tier cEDH decks! You can still get great results from an off meta build!
- Mnemonic Betrayal is an even more powerful example of this idea—since decks often share so many cards, in a lot of games it effectively becomes a better Yawgmoth's Will. Praetor's Grasp is commonly played in Ad Nauseam decks, because as long as there's at least one similar deck at the table, it's effectively a Grim Tutor with upside.
- The more applicable lesson to be taken from cEDH is the card quality filter—running cards that are more flexible and more efficient, cards that consistently feel at least decent to draw, will improve your deck. Basically, minimize dead draws, and if cards rely on other cards to be good, their effect needs to be very strong to make up for the amount of time they'll sit dead in hand.
