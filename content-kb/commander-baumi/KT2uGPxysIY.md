## Summary

This video documents an experiment to play the oldest known EDH/Commander deck lists to understand the format's early history firsthand, using proxies and original card versions under old rules (shuffling the commander into the deck, no command zone). Sheldon Menery's 2004 Arcades Sabboth deck proves nearly unplayable today: extremely high average mana value, almost no ramp beyond five-mana rocks, only 37 lands, and minimal real card draw, so games often stall on land-pass turns. Its plan—cheat out huge creatures via Defense of the Heart, Tooth and Nail, and Thicket Elemental—shines only when it works, delivering battlecruiser fun but no momentum or engine. Nathan Long's contest-winning Lord of Tresserhorn reanimator deck is far stronger: built around regeneration, removal (about a fifth of the deck), tutors, and recursion loops (Lifeline plus Crater Hellion). Regenerate proves polarizing—broken when opponents lack removal that bypasses it, useless otherwise. The narrator concludes early EDH mainly differed in card quality and power creep, not player mentality, and argues Sol Ring's iconic status stems from helping decks skip sluggish early games. Old Magic remains playable, which he finds beautiful.

## Key Clips

- In 2004 Sheldon Menery wrote a blog post on Star City Games that introduced the world to Elder Dragon Highlander. He explained the rules of the format and also showcased his own Arcades Sabboth deck, the first EDH deck list.
- The actual cards in Menery's Arcades Sabboth deck make it fundamentally different from modern Commander decks: its average mana value is incredibly high, there are hardly any creatures that cost less than five mana, and the deck hardly plays any ramp aside from five-mana rocks. Missing a land drop might as well be GG.
- One of the core strategies of the deck is to use cards like Defense of the Heart, Thicket Elemental, and Tooth and Nail to cheat out enormously high-cost creatures. There are also graveyard synergies but no ways to fill up the yard.
- To learn about EDH's past, I found the oldest EDH deck lists available, made proxies, and assembled the decks. I played with the original old EDH rules: no command zone, instead shuffling the commander into the deck at the start of the game.
- In 2004 Menery ran a deck-building contest using Lord of Tresserhorn as the general, won by Nathan Long. Lord of Tresserhorn is a four-mana Grixis 10/4 that regenerates for a single black mana, but on entry you pay two life, sacrifice two creatures, and an opponent draws two cards.
- The Lord of Tresserhorn list is fully designed around its commander with regeneration, removal to clear blockers, and 10-damage attacks. The biggest weakness is no card draw — the best draw card is Decree of Pain, which destroys all creatures and they can't be regenerated, killing your own Lord.
- Regenerate is a problematic, rightfully-retired mechanic: when opponents lack removal that bypasses it (swords to plowshares, pongify, terminate, damnation, etc.) it becomes broken — you attack freely for 10 and there's nothing they can do, but against bypass removal it does literally nothing.
- Takeaway on Sol Ring: ancient EDH decks ran many tutors because they had to find their general from a low-quality card pool, so they doubled down to always find Sol Ring and bypass a sluggish early game. That context explains why Sol Ring became the icon, even if it has no place in modern Commander decks.
