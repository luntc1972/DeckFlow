## Summary

The video argues against upgrading an EDH/Commander deck past a healthy mid power level, giving reasons illustrated with the creator's own decks. First, some decks have bottlenecks card quality can't fix: heavy reliance on a >3-mana commander, graveyard buildup, or board wipes/goad makes them vulnerable to targeted hate that grows more common at higher power (Sharuum Slide—Astral Slides, artifacts, graveyard all exploitable). Second, the "cheap deck" scales logarithmically: the best low-cost pieces are already run, so upgrading means cutting core cards for generic value, weakening the plan (Feather, Meria with 1-2 mana spells/equipment). Third, you may simply dislike the resulting power level (Glissa kept mid to do battlecruiser things, not sneaky combos). Power has two factors: card quality and cohesion; players focus on card quality because it's a clean money-for-power trade, while cohesion is hard to modulate on a built deck. Treat card quality as a tool to reach a desired power level. Upgradability depends on a strong, flexible resource-generating core: Yuriko (2-mana ninjutsu converting evasive creatures/ninjas into card advantage and life loss) upgrades far better than Feather's worse, less flexible core. Mid-power commanders are fine—just know the cost and watch weak points against stronger decks.

## Key Clips

- **[01:00]** The first reason not to upgrade a deck is that some decks have natural bottlenecks in their functionality that makes it difficult to give them a cohesive upgrade, and the most common bottlenecks here are being vulnerable to a particular type of hate or having a game plan that is fundamentally difficult to speed up.
- **[01:14]** If your deck relies heavily on the board presence of a commander that's more than three mana, your opponents only need to hate out your command zone a couple times early in order to shut you down. If your deck heavily relies on building up a graveyard, a single piece of graveyard hate can totally blow you out, and card quality improvements alone won't generally change this fact.
- **[02:31]** Trying to improve a deck with a bottleneck like this will generally lead to some very good games with the deck, but much worse consistency, with the deck getting dragged down to the dirt by its bottlenecking factors a decent portion of the time.
- **[02:35]** An extension on this problem is what I'll call 'the cheap deck'. This is a deck that works pretty well at low budgets, but is very logarithmic in terms of how it translates additional budget to additional power—it gets rapidly more expensive to improve past a certain power level.
- **[03:38]** The other reason not to increase the power level of a deck is if you simply don't enjoy the power level you end up at. Even if you're still pulling off your fair share of wins, as a player you need to ask yourself the question of if that's the sort of MTG you want to be playing.
- **[05:04]** There are two main factors that determine a deck's power level: card quality—how good the cards are, and cohesion—how well the cards are put together. We as magic players tend to overwhelmingly focus on the first one when we think about upgrading.
- **[05:40]** You can think of upgrading your deck's card quality as a tool for obtaining the deck's desired power level. If you have a fundamentally powerful deck concept and you want to play in a group of more middling power decks, you can restrict your card choice by budget or theme or some other factor.
- **[07:54]** It's this strong core for generating generic resources that makes a deck especially upgradable—there are lots of piecemeal card quality upgrades and packages that can be added to modulate the deck to whatever power level the player wants to be playing at. By contrast, the core of a Feather deck is worse and less flexible overall.
