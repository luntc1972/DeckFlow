## Key Clips

- **[02:08]** A deck should have an idea of roughly what specific boardstate it would like to create, when, and how that boardstate will lead to victory. Otherwise it is a moodboard of the most aesthetic-looking threshold creatures.
- **[02:55]** Widening your on-ramp: the gameplan is a bucket the deck fires tennis balls at. Randomness is unavoidable, but deckbuilding choices control the size of the bucket. Missing land drops are a large part of the misses, especially with a commander costing four or more, so adding lands to a land-light deck widens it.
- **[03:36]** Keeping payoffs lower-costed and more broadly useful widens the on-ramp. High-cost amplifiers raise peak power but don't help you jump into your gameplan, so each one narrows the on-ramp. Make sure a relatively wide portion of your cards are helpful early.
- **[04:07]** If your deck isn't working, the first suggestion is always to add more draw and filtering. A ton of EDH decks aren't running enough draw, and it solves the problem a lot of the time.
- **[04:26]** Card draw also helps identify what is working. A bad card can stay in the deck if you see it once every four games, but at one in three or one in two you face its issues more often. Likewise a surprise powerhouse becomes clear when drawn more.
- **[05:54]** Whether opponents want to play against a deck isn't all-or-nothing. A deck more irritating to play against may get played against less often, which is a failure if your goal is an everyday default deck.
- **[09:39]** Key to the fourth failure state is expectations: have an idea of how often you expect to play a deck and how long you expect it to stay fresh. No deck is a failure if you have the right mindset for it.
