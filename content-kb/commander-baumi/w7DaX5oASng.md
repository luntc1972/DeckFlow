## Summary

This video ranks the five best (not strongest) Final Fantasy Commander cards, judged by fun, design, and deckbuilding implications. Edgar, King of Figaro is a monoblue 6-mana commander praised as a 99-card coin-flip enabler and a strong monoblue artifact-shell leader that draws cards per artifact; keeping him monoblue is called smart design. Cipher Almazi, a monored 4-mana 3/4, grants double strike to a lone attacker and recasts cheap instants/sorceries from the graveyard on combat damage, blending Voltron and spellslinger with burn potential. Joshua, Phoenix's Dominant, a Boros 3/4, rummages on entry and flips into a saga Phoenix that burns opponents, gains life via lifelink, and reanimates creatures totaling six mana value; deliberately fair and low-threat. Ardyn the Usurper, a monoblack 8-mana 4/4, gives demons menace/lifelink/haste and reanimates graveyard creatures as demon tokens—weak but a cool late-game finisher. The Wandering Minstrel, a 2-mana Simic commander, makes lands enter untapped (a powerful ramp engine), creates tokens with five-plus towns, and offers a repeatable instant-speed overrun, deemed absurdly powerful. The creator also shouts out Squall and his own Choco/Chocobo deck.

## Key Clips

- Edgar, King of Figaro is a six-mana mono-blue 4/5 with two abilities. When Edgar enters, we draw a card for each artifact we control. And the first time each turn we flip one or more coins, those coins come up heads and we win those flips. But there are no mono-blue cards that let us flip coins — zero. So Edgar isn't meant to be the commander of a coin-flipping deck; he's meant to be in the 99 of one.
- By keeping Edgar mono-blue, Wizards enforced something I'm always advocating: playing game-warping commanders in the 99 as opposed to the command zone. As the leader of a mono-blue artifact shell he'd perform excellently — artifact decks quickly bring permanents into play but burn resources doing so, and casting Edgar is easy in those decks and then he'll draw like 10 cards.
- Cipher Almazi is a four-mana mono-red 3/4. Whenever a creature we control attacks alone it gains double strike, and whenever Cipher deals combat damage to a player we may cast a target instant or sorcery with mana value three or less from our graveyard for free. Double strike lets Cipher deal combat damage twice, triggering the recast each time — a fascinating mix between Voltron and mono-red spellslinger.
- Joshua, Phoenix's Dominant is a three-mana Boros 3/4. When he enters we discard up to two cards and draw that many. For five mana we tap him to exile and transform into Phoenix, Warden of Fire — a flying lifelink saga. Chapters one and two deal two damage to each opponent (healing us via lifelink), and chapter three reanimates any number of creatures with combined mana value six or less, then transforms back into Joshua.
- Ardan the Usurper is an eight-mana mono-black 4/4 that gives all our demons menace, lifelink, and haste, and at the beginning of combat lets us exile a creature card from any graveyard and create a token copy as a 5/5 black demon. He isn't strong — an eight-mana 4/4 without protection — but it's a sick way to end the game. Every Ardan deck will look different, allowing for great creative expression.
- The Wandering Minstrel is a two-mana Simic 1/3 that makes all our lands enter untapped. Having lands enter untapped on a two-mana commander is incredibly powerful — we become freed from mortal ramp, can play every two-mana land, slam depletion and bounce lands tapping for two right away. It's the best ramp commander, and then they also gave him a repeatable instant-speed overrun for eight mana that pumps all creatures by the number of towns we control.
- This video isn't about the strongest Final Fantasy commanders. Many of the strongest commanders are basically unplayable in a social-first environment because they take over the game in a way that's frustrating for everybody else. So I'm here to tell you about the five best — entirely dependent on how fun I think they'll be to play and play against, how much I like their design, and how evocative their deck-building implications are.
