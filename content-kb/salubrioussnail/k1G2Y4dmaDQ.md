## Summary

Review of the Blame Game commander precon, led by Nelly. Nelly's first ability suspects a creature (disabling a blocker for evasion) and goads, pushing opponents to attack each other; the second ability draws up to three cards per turn cycle while feeding opponents cards for hitting each other, discouraging removal. The deck steers the midgame by making opponents fight amongst themselves, drawing cards off it, then closing with beefy creatures. Fundamentals are decent: ~8 draw, 11 ramp, 9 removal (mostly high-cost), 10 extra goad cards. Three core problems: (1) 33 player-protective cards is far too many slots for an effect that can't win on its own, leaving dead hands against non-combat wincons; (2) bad manabaseΓÇö37 lands but 11 colorless-only utility lands yield just 20 white/17 red sources, starving double-red costs like Vengeful Ancestor; (3) weak curveΓÇöfew good three-drops, no two-drops besides rocks. Across 15 playtests it curved out 8 times via aggressive mulligans. Tuning: cut excess defensive cards, add haste and board protection for Nelly; swap colorless lands/rocks for colored sources; either keep the high curve with more two-cost rocks (option A, chosen) or lower the curve with cheap creatures (option B). Goal: swap 20 cards, keep budget and spirit.

## Key Clips

- Nelly's first ability contains the 'suspect' component, which offers immediate evasion by disabling a blocker, as well as the goad component, which offers a way to keep threats away from our life total as long as Nelly keeps attacking, and guarantees that our opponents will be attacking each other. The second ability is a source of draw that can offer up to three cards per turn cycle, while also feeding opponents that resource as a reward for hitting each other.
- Basically, this deck is trying to control the midgame by getting our opponents to fight amongst themselves, and Nelly draws us additional cards when they do this. This card draw should hopefully fuel a powerful late game, where a suite of bigger creatures means that once our opponents are dropping in life total and dwindling in number, we can take control of the game with our boardstate.
- I tallied up all the cards that discourage or disallow attacking us in some way, and the total for these was 33 cards. One third of the deck is trying to get our opponents to attack each other instead of us. 33 deck slots is way too many for a task that won't win or prevent our opponents from winning on its own.
- The manabase is bad. 37 lands might be an okay count in the abstract, but it includes 11 solely-colorless-producing utility lands. This results in the deck having 20 white sources and 17 red sources between its artifacts and lands. And this deck has like 10 double red cards in itΓÇöyou need 26 red sources to consistently cast a Vengeful Ancestor on curve, and here we are with 17.
- Another issue that was on my radar but which became much more clear in playtesting was that the deck has only a handful of good three drops, and basically no two drops other than mana rocks. Leaving your two drop slot on the table in a two color deck is not good and will hurt your chances of winning.
- The three standout issues being manabase, mana curve, and an excess of player-protective cards. For improving this deck, I want to maintain the affordability and spirit of the deck, but smooth out some of the rough edges. First of all, I would cut a bunch of defensive cards. We want several more symbols of each color, and to get there we cut colorless lands and rocks for ones that produce colored mana.
- Option A would be to keep the higher curve, add a couple lands, and add 4-5 two cost mana rocks to make sure we're able to swing with the commander on turn 4 as often as possible. Option B would be to drop the curve, and add a bunch of 2 and 3 drop creatures that could lead us to having a more aggressive gameplan. In my list I went for option A, as it seemed more in-line with the original plan of the deck.
