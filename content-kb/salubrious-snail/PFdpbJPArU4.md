## Summary

The creator, a self-described EDH 'hipster' who loves weird, unusual decks, examines a nagging doubt: whether playing funky, unique decks is actually good for the format. EDH grew from a casual pickup format (championed by Sheldon Menery, 2002-2011) into Wizards' de-facto casual format, with precons making it one-size-fits-all. The core problem is information overload, split into card diversity and gameplay diversity. With tens of thousands of singleton cards across four players, and Wizards now printing thousands of new cards yearly, even experts struggle to track opponents' plansΓÇöand new players face ten times the unique cards of a 60-card format. This worsens as decks become streamlined and at high-power casual, where hidden combos and value engines make threat assessment a luck check. The creator's beloved $25 ten-deck pool worked because plans became near-public knowledge and interaction-dense control kept boards clean. Solutions: explain your deck while shuffling, verbally flag game-ending spells (Scapeshift vs Llanowar Elves), keep revealed cards visible, reject deception, and run interaction-dense, low-to-the-ground decks. But reducing format variety isn't the answerΓÇöEDH's creative breadth (Adam Staley's original vision) is its point; mitigate the overload instead.

## Key Clips

- The format has a card selection that's almost as wide as Vintage's, it has thousands of different options for commanders, and the fact that it doesn't have a single set power level means that no deck is fully squeezed out as long as you're willing to shop around for an appropriate table to play at.
- Pickup EDH can be an absolute information overload, and this broader problem can be broken down into two separate sub-problems: card diversity, and gameplay diversity.
- Since the format is 100-card singleton, seeing any singular card in a deck gives far fewer implications for what the rest of the deck might contain compared to other formats, and also results in far more unique cards in each deck. Multiply this by three to account for the multiplayer aspect of the format, and there will be a lot of cards to keep track of just as a baseline.
- When I made my video about cEDH a few months back, I got a number of interesting comments from people who prefer competitive EDH specifically because there are fewer viable cards in the format that require keeping track of. I see this as the most reasonable response to my main gripe with cEDHΓÇö"yes, fewer cards are viable, and that's good, actually."
- It's the power level I'd describe as high power casual where things start to feel really weird when playing with strangers. Infinite combos, locks, and back-breakingly-powerful value engines get assembled on a whim and with only relatively subtle hints to indicate their approach. High power casual is packed to the rafters with off-meta, because there isn't a meaningful meta beyond some archetypes being a bit more popular than others.
- If I know my opponents' decks, I can think to myself "okay, I'm 90% sure player A doesn't have the pieces to combo off on their next turn." But, if instead of being 85% sure it's safe to tap out I have no firm estimate whatsoever, what exactly am I supposed to do? Hold up mana every turn? On some level that's just a random luck check for whether or not I lose to some bullshit.
- From fall of 2019 to summer of 2021 my playgroup of five people played about 200 games with a set of ten budget decks. The fact that we were exclusively playing with these ten guild-divided decks meant that the plans for each deck and the cards used within those plans became all but public knowledge. The other big thing that made the $25 deck pool so good for avoiding information overload was that the decks were interaction dense, as well as low-slung and grindy.
- Whenever I play with new people at my local game store I'll generally give some explanation of the deck I'm playing while I'm shuffling up, and I'll often ask for the same from my opponents. A relatively basic method is verbally differentiating commonly-game-ending spells from other spells. For example, saying "I play a Llanowar Elves" versus "I attempt to cast Scapeshift."
