## Summary

The creator argues Commander damage is a poorly implemented rule and proposes reworking rather than removing it. Main problem: it forces tracking a separate health bar per commander (up to 8+ with partners), pushing complexity past the point where physical play needs a digital device—undermining Magic's physicality and overwhelming new players, who assume health is intuitive. He critiques the 21 threshold as awkward (only divides by 3 and 7); 20 would be cleaner and more intuitive. Commander damage rarely matters since modern commanders are value engines, not 7-power beaters, so it consumes complexity budget while seldom activating. It's also parasitic—only a commander deals its own commander damage, with no synergy from other creatures or players—and it encourages targeting one player repeatedly, harming the social, spread-the-damage nature of multiplayer. He dismisses the life-gain-deck justification as a social/communication issue. Three fixes proposed: (1) one shared commander-damage pool per player (threshold ~40); (2) commander damage as a win condition accumulating toward ~40; (3) his favorite—commanders deal double combat damage to players, removing extra trackers. Playtesting the third made combat dynamic and fun; he currently plays with Commander damage off.

## Key Clips

- Commander damage makes it so that whenever a commander deals a total of 21 or more combat damage to a player, that player loses the game. The speaker argues it is a poorly implemented rule that causes more problems than benefits, though the core idea is worth preserving.
- Visible game complexity is a limited resource — complexity is a budget. For a game like Magic that is already very complicated, you want the lowest possible base-game rules complexity so there is room for rules complexity on individual cards without players feeling overwhelmed.
- In a game of Commander every player effectively has five life bars: 40 life plus a separate health bar for each player's commander, all tracked separately. With partner commanders this can reach eight additional health bars per player, pushing complexity to where a digital device becomes necessary — violating Magic's strength of physicality.
- The 21 threshold is awkward: 21 only divides into three and seven, seemingly chosen to minimize how many creatures reach it. It traces back to the original Elder Dragons (seven power each) where three attacks equaled a kill. 20 would be more intuitive — half the starting life total — and divides into 2, 4, 5, and 10.
- Commander damage kills are rare; most 2024 commanders are value engines or enablers, not 7/7 beaters. So the rule usually consumes complexity budget without coming into play, yet still must be tracked just in case — a complicated rule that seldom matters.
- Commander damage is a parasitic mechanic: only your commander can deal it, nothing else synergizes with it, and other players can't finish off someone you dealt 20 commander damage to. Voltron decks lean into it but are famously fragile because losing the commander removes their only damage source.
- The rule encourages targeting individuals: once you've stacked commander damage on one opponent you're incentivized to keep attacking that same player regardless of board state. This creates social friction — early knockouts and hostility — compromising Commander's social design pillar.
- The speaker's favorite proposed fix: stop tracking commander damage as a separate value; instead whenever a player loses life from a commander's combat damage, they lose that much life again (commanders deal double combat damage to players, without doubling LifeLink or combat triggers). Playtested locally — commanders felt powerful, combat became highly interactive, and games were easier to track with only four numbers.
