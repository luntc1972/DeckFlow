## Summary

Argues that playing non-EDH formats (20-life 1v1, Pauper, draft, sealed/prerelease, cube) sharpens commander skills. Gameplay lessons: treat life total as a resource, block with useful creatures when needed to survive, and stay engaged in low-resource/topdeck or stax-attrition games instead of conceding. Deckbuilding lessons center on ramp: in 60-card and limited formats ramp is viewed critically ("why is this here?") and cut if unjustified, whereas EDH players auto-include 8-10 ramp slots (e.g. Cultivate in two-color decks with a four-mana commander, Gilded Lotus in a 2.5-average-MV deck, Rampant Growth in an artifact deck with a three-mana commander) without asking what it accomplishes. Recommends templates (low-double-digit draw, low-double-digit ramp) as flexible starting points, not gospel. Notes some decks want little ramp: his Malfegor and Francisco/Malcolm decks run only a low single-digit count of two-mana rocks (to avoid drawing multiples early) plus a Sol Ring, preferring to develop more useful cards on turns 2-3. Same critical lens applies to card draw and board wipes—not always necessities. Also stresses that EDH poorly teaches optimal card quantities since singleton caps copies; players over-load effects when more copies are available. Cites Llanowar Elves, Dance of the Tumbleweeds, Poison Dart Frog as limited ramp examples.

## Key Clips

- **[03:31]** Playing commander gives lots of experience fighting big bombastic wincons—combo decks, a massive X spell, a Craterhoof on a board of tokens—so life total often feels irrelevant, meaning commander players are less prepared for grindier games where every point of life matters.
- **[04:00]** Players experienced in formats where life totals matter are more careful of life-total thresholds and more willing to block to stay above them; they internalize that life total is a resource, knowing when to spend and when to save it.
- **[05:02]** When a game involves stax or heavy attrition and resources dwindle, you can tell who has played other formats—EDH-only players treat it as a lost cause, while limited/60-card players stay vigilant, looking for outs and incremental advantage.
- **[05:29]** In formats outside commander, ramp is viewed critically—a player must ask 'why is this here?', and if there's no clear answer the card gets cut. Many EDH players instead just add the requisite ramp count from staples without considering what it accomplishes.
- **[05:53]** Examples of thoughtless ramp: Cultivate in two-color decks with a four-mana commander, Gilded Lotus in decks averaging 2.5 mana value, Rampant Growth in an artifact deck with a three-mana commander.
- **[08:56]** In his Malfegor and Francisco/Malcolm decks he doesn't want to spend turns 2 or 3 on ramp because he has more useful cards to develop; each runs only a low single-digit count of two-mana rocks so as not to draw more than one early, plus a Sol Ring.
- **[09:27]** The same critical questions apply to other card types—when is it correct to go light on or exclude card draw, or exclude board wipes? EDH players treat these as necessities, but depending on the deck they may not be.
- **[10:02]** Singleton restricts copies, so EDH outsources quantity decisions to the format; players who've only played EDH tend to load up on all available copies of an appealing effect regardless of how many are actually optimal.
