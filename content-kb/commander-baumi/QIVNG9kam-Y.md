## Summary

The video argues against the common Commander advice to fix struggling decks by adding more removal. While the creator acknowledges that spamming sweepers (especially Planar Cleansing and partially one-sided wipes) is genuinely effective at winning casual social CommanderΓÇöbecause those decks rely on permanent synergies and artifact mana that punishing wipes disruptΓÇöhe warns that stacking 20 pieces of interaction with no wincon produces slow, unsatisfying games that drag to a resignation rather than a climax. He reframes Commander as a social, cooperative game (more Mario Party than chess) where a positive experience for all players matters more than win rate. His prescription: defocus winning and instead build toward finishes. Every deck should run three to five individually powerful, game-ending cardsΓÇöoverruns like Akroma's/Aurelia-style forerunners, damage amplifiers like City on Fire, mill like Maze's End-style monuments, Doomsday Excruciator, or Apex of PowerΓÇörather than relying on combos. He caps his own decks at about three sweepers and three to five targeted removal spells, keeping interaction healthy but limited, forcing a proactive win path. Removal isn't wrong, but finishes create the flashy, narrative-satisfying endings that make games worth retelling.

## Key Clips

- If you're struggling in Commander, the best answer is to include more removal to deal with the problems. This is not a fundamentally wrong idea. One of the best, most effective ways of winning in commander is to just play a ton of removal, particularly sweepers.
- I firmly believe that Planar Cleansing and its iterations are the strongest cards you can play in casual social commander because the decks are usually centered around extended synergies between permanents and players rely heavily on artifacts for mana. So getting everything destroyed will force them into a state of rebuilding for multiple turns.
- New players absorb 'play more removal' and end up with decks stacked with 20 pieces of interaction and no way to win before the end of the night. Those games are usually unsatisfying for everybody involved because instead of ending in a grand finale, they slowly drag themselves to the finish line.
- Commander is a lot more Mario Party than it is chess. We care about the time spent, about the little moments we share with the players around us. Something that really helped me elevate my enjoyment of Commander was defocusing winning as the goal of the game.
- Instead of focusing so much on removal, I think it's better to focus on finishes. Every deck should play at least three to five cards that were chosen purely to end the game. Not combos, but individually powerful cards that represent a game-winning threat ΓÇö classic overruns like Aetherflux, damage amplifiers like City on Fire, massive mill like Riverchurn Monument, or Apex of Power. Commander is the big spell format.
- I usually play a maximum of three sweepers per deck and around three to five pieces of targeted interaction, mostly erring on the lower end. This means I tend to see two to four of these cards per game, which is enough for it to feel interactive, but not enough to use them on any small problem that I come across. My path to victory has to be a proactive one.
- At its core, Commander is a cooperative game. We shouldn't try to keep the other players hostage, but to have a good time together. Winning should be secondary; our actual aim should be a positive game experience for all players. Instead of just surviving, we should be trying to end the game ourselves.
- There's a bit of a problem: what I said assumes that the other players cooperate. If I'm not playing a pile of removal, and then everybody else just plays the nastiest, fastest synergies imaginable, then we have a different problem. But if we can all keep ourselves in check and focus on the experience around us, I think Commander can be absolutely wonderful.
