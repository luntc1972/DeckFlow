## Summary

This deck is a Sharuum Esper (white-blue-black) cycling control deck built around Astral Slide, which flickers a creature each time a card is cycled to reuse ETBs and remove threats temporarily. It runs a high-30s cycling-card count plus all 14 Esper cycling lands to reliably trigger Slide, at least 30 creatures to enable Oversold Cemetery's recursion, and seven draw-and-discard effects to feed the graveyard. Card slots are scarce, so every card must fill multiple roles or excel at one. A toolbox of conditional tutors—artifact mages (Tribute, Treasure, Sphinx Summoner finding Fluctuator and top-end artifacts), Wizardcycling, Zur, and Transmute cards—lets it assemble Astral Slide, Fluctuator, or Oversold Cemetery from many angles. Play is control: balance staying alive, suppressing opponents, and building value, repeating removal and ETBs over turns toward a stable end-game of mana, blockers, cards, and high life. Finishers are evasive fliers plus Magister Sphinx and Herald of the Forgotten. The deck defends Sharuum as a support piece and argues conditional tutors increase deck diversity. Resilience comes from interconnected synergy packages, stifle effects against graveyard hate, and easy pivots between value engines.

## Key Clips

- The starting point was browsing a card gallery of Onslaught cards. Astral Slide, a three mana white enchantment that flickers a creature whenever a card is cycled, offers an effective way to reuse ETBs and temporarily remove opposing creatures. Oversold Cemetery, a two mana black enchantment, returns a creature from its controller's graveyard to their hand on each upkeep as long as there are four or more creatures in that graveyard—a recursion engine enabled by cycling.
- I settled on Sharuum as an Esper commander with an impactful graveyard-oriented ETB. The deck needs a high-30s count of cycling cards for reliable Astral Slide triggers, a creature count of at least 30 for Oversold Cemetery, tutors to find the three Astral Slide-style enchantments, and draw-and-discard effects to feed the graveyard. Even running all 14 cycling lands in Esper colors leaves under 40 slots for nonland, non-cycling cards—so cards must perform multiple roles or be utterly fantastic at their main job.
- This is a toolbox deck—a network of conditional tutors that each find a certain selection of cards for various situations. Zur tutors out low-cost enchantments when he attacks and is also a Wizard, which connects to Wizardcycling: instant-speed tutors that also count as a cycle for Astral Slide. Transmute cards let you pay three mana and discard them to tutor for a card of the same mana cost. The cumulative effect: six cards can lead to one of the three key Astral Slide enchantments, and five cards each can find Fluctuator or Oversold Cemetery.
- This deck is a control deck through and through, despite a high creature count of 35 and no conventional board wipe. At any moment it balances three goals—stay alive, suppress opponents, and build value. A big part of the gameplan is repeating value generation and removal via Astral Slide flickers, with repeated removal acting as heavy-duty hate over multiple turn cycles. Since it's a toolbox deck, every suppression tool you don't need to grab becomes a piece of value or ramp instead.
- Finishers: Magister Sphinx sets a problem player's life total to 10—discard it to a draw/discard effect, cycle to flicker Sharuum and bring it back, then clear flying blockers and one-shot the opponent. Herald of the Forgotten brings back any of the 14 cycling creatures and any of the 14 cycling lands from the graveyard—a burst of blockers plus a burst of ramp that fuels both staying alive and building value.
- Resiliency comes from the deck's many interconnected themes. Astral Slide effects build value with just an ETB creature even without a graveyard, since the high density of cycling cards keeps the engine running. The deck has rare good access to stifle effects to deal with graveyard hate. Most game actions are abilities rather than spells, insulating it against conventional control hate, and conditional tutors let you avoid tutoring for an effect that's currently being hated on.
- On tutors: conditional tutors actively make the deck more interesting because they let you run a greater variety of cards that interact in complex ways and find cards that aren't great without support from core cards. Through tutors you run very little generic ramp, draw, and removal—card choices get more specific to the deck. The objection that having a 'secret commander' goes against the spirit of EDH falls apart: if coordinating cards around a card you play every game is wrong, then EDH goes against the spirit of EDH.
- Building a hyper-connected synergy machine is a slow, iterative process. The most important part is finding nodes that pull different elements together—Wizardcycling was the big one—then including only cards that perform multiple roles or perform their primary role fantastically well. It requires restraint: resist throwing cards in for a single idealized situation, and ask what a card looks like in the less ideal half of situations. Weed out cards that share a synergy pillar but aren't actually good, like Curator of Mysteries or Abandoned Sarcophagus.
