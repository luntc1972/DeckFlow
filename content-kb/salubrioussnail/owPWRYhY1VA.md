## Summary

This video argues that a powerful, mechanically-fitting card can still be the wrong inclusion in a casual EDH deck if it "shortcuts the fun." Casual decks are intentionally non-optimal, so players should optimize for a specific feeling rather than raw power. The creator illustrates with four decks. In Malfegor, Kroxa fits a grindy low-resource gameplan while Waste Not is cut because it hands easy resources that lift the deck out of its intended scrappy state. In Sharuum Astral Slide, a control deck built on intricate flicker/tutor decisions, Monument to Endurance (plus draw-doublers like Alhammarett's Archive and Teferi's Ageless Insight) is excluded for collapsing rich value choices into a do-thing-get-cards engine. In the Radha deck, simple strong draw is welcome because building advantage isn't the point—big mana and beating down are. In Hans' Lilah "prophetic bolt" deck, Thousand Year Storm is cut for negating both card and mana value, replaced by tamer amplifiers (Swarm Intelligence, new Niv-Mizzet). The advice: identify what you enjoy—resource-building, interaction, earned wins—and avoid cards that accomplish those tasks too efficiently or win prematurely. Running weaker, niche tools engages more deckbuilding muscles and makes wins feel more satisfying.

## Key Clips

- Commander, as it is played by most of its playerbase, is a casual format... a deck is casual when it is not perfectly optimal in terms of its ability to perform tasks and win games, and when this state of being is intentional. If you are a casual EDH player, by definition there are cards in your deck that could be switched to different cards if you wanted to win more games.
- There are some good cards that, like a Dualcaster-Twinflame combo, may influence the amount of fun you have with the game, cards that may not even necessarily have anything directly to do with winning. What you find fun about playing your deck may be a lot more fragile than you think, much more sensitive to the blunt instrument of an out of place card or two than you'd expect.
- Kroxa is very good at doing everything this Malfegor deck wants—making opponents discard, being resilient, and applying life total pressure. Waste Not did not end up in the final deck because it is good at things the deck is not supposed to be good at. This is a grindy mid-power deck looking to plunge the game into a low-resource slug fest, and Waste Not handing me easy resources boosts me out of that state.
- The issue with Monument to Endurance is that it shortcuts around this whole thing. Its ability doesn't compete with anything else in the deck, and the correct first choice per turn will almost always be card draw. This simplification of value generation decisions has a cascading effect of simplifying my tutoring decisions—it will almost always be correct to hunt up a one-piece engine like this. These do-thing-get-cards synergies would suck the identity and a lot of the interesting decisions out of the deck.
- Thousand Year Storm effectively nullifies value as a concept in the deck—the deck is likely adding ten or more cards to its hand each turn that Thousand Year Storm is in play. Worse, TYS negates the concept of mana as well—when a five or six cost card can be copied half a dozen times, the progression of adding on mana rocks and amplifiers becomes entirely pointless. It was replaced by Swarm Intelligence; the difference between a 2x amplification and a 3-4x amplification ends up being a lot more tangible than you'd expect.
- If there are a specific set of interactions that make a deck fun and meaningful to you, the worst possible thing you can do is add a different set of cards that does the same thing better, and in a less interesting way. If you have a deck that focuses on dungeons and the initiative, adding in a Rhystic Study and a Trouble in Pairs will probably improve the deck, but suddenly you might find these unrelated cards outshining the set of value engines you built your whole deck around.
- The solution isn't even necessarily to abandon the optimization instinct, but rather to optimize for something that isn't raw power level. You can think of it as optimizing for fun, but it's going to be easier to tackle if you identify some more specific goal. For me, that's building a deck that embodies a specific feeling through its design, a specific vibe.
- Not having access to a key mana engine, Krark Clan Ironworks, forced the $25 eggs deck to traverse its explosive storm gameplan in much more intentional and dynamic ways than would be required if it did have access to KCI. Playing with weaker connecting material or payoffs can really bring out some more fun and textured elements from explosive decks which might otherwise effortlessly combo off and win.
