## Summary

The creator reviews Magic: The Gathering Commander's new bracket system, which replaces the old power-level framework with five brackets and a 'Game Changers' list of exceedingly powerful cards (Smothering Tithe, Rhystic Study, Demonic Tutor). They praise the shift away from quantifying power levels toward communicating intent, calling it more accessible. They argue Sol Ring belongs on the Game Changer list—it shapes games and they cut it from every deck—but Wizards excludes it on tradition, which they find a weak emotional justification. Bracket-by-bracket: 1 (Exhibition) is joke decks, 2 (Core) is precon-level, 3 (Upgraded) allows up to three Game Changers and two-card combos, 4 (Optimized) is high-power, 5 is cEDH. Their main criticism: a gap exists between brackets 2 and 3—they want stronger-than-precon decks without Game Changers (a '2.5'), and propose banning Game Changers from bracket 3 to clarify intent. They also criticize Moxfield/Scryfall auto-categorizing decks, since machines can't know intent; all their decks were wrongly labeled bracket 2. Overall they find the system solid but needing work, and remain optimistic it's fixable.

## Key Clips

- The new bracket system, which is notably not called Power brackets anymore, introduces the concept of Game Changers: a list of exceedingly powerful cards that shape a Commander game when they are played, cards like Smothering Tithe, Rhystic Study, and Demonic Tutor. The game changer list is then used to help define the brackets.
- Commander's rule zero problem isn't a power level problem but a communication problem. Power levels are incredibly difficult to quantify, and players perceive power levels differently from each other, so it's not a useful metric. If we communicate what kind of experience is desired, then everybody can work toward that goal in their own way.
- Sol Ring should be on the game changer list. It's the strongest card in the format and it drastically shapes games around itself when it's played. Wizards acknowledges it's powerful enough to be considered a game changer, but makes an emotional call on tradition to justify its exclusion. Sol Ring is incredibly easy to replace — just put in a basic land.
- Bracket 1 (Exhibition) is about joke decks. Bracket 2 (Core) is the average power level of a modern precon. Bracket 3 (Upgraded) is stronger than precons but not fully optimized, and may include up to three game changers and two-card combos so long as they can't come down early. Bracket 4 is optimized high-powered decks with no restriction other than the ban list. Bracket 5 is cEDH.
- When you tell people they can only do something three times, even if they weren't doing it before, they will now do it three times. They will think of it as a budget and plan how to best use those three opportunities. Allowing three game changers in bracket 3 creates that incentive.
- The best Commander experience excludes game changers but takes place at a distinctly higher power level than precons. The bracket system doesn't account for this — there's a missing bracket between 2 and 3, a '2.5' that is stronger than precons but plays no game changers. The better solution is to just ban game changers from bracket 3 to create a clearer divide between brackets three and four.
- The difference between brackets 4 and 5: in bracket 4 your deck still has a theme, just pushed as far as it can go with the strongest cards available. In bracket 5, winning is the theme — the only criteria for inclusion is whether it increases your chance of winning. As people put it: if you have to ask, then it's not cEDH.
- Having tools like Moxfield automatically detect brackets is missing the point. Brackets are a communication tool about intent, not power — the machine can't know your intent, it can only count how many game changers are in a deck. Slapping a default label on every deck makes players engage only on a surface level. If they want automatic integration, they should make it a field constantly asking what your intent is until you fill it in.
