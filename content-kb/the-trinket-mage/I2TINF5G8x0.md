## Summary

This Magic Mirror podcast episode covers Strixhaven pre-release impressions and audience hot takes on Commander (EDH) strategy. The hosts dislike seated sealed pre-release for limiting deckbuilding but enjoyed Prismari pools; they rate fix-what's-broken among the worst limited cards and praise uncommons like Muse Seeker. Key strategic positions: attack defenseless players because EDH life totals are too high and empty boards often signal an imminent combo or a greedy land-light keep. Stax suffers only because Wizards under-supports it. Partner is balanced by specific designs, not the mechanic; niche low-power partners (Tormod, Livio) are fine, while Timna and two-color partners are problematic. Bracket 3 isn't a real formatΓÇöbuild for fast interaction, not game-changer count; Bola's Citadel and Smothering Tithe are the likeliest runaway cards. Lightning Greaves is an average staple (overplayed at ~25%), strong only when you need both haste and protection. Players over-rely on generic board wipes; prefer one-sided or partial wipes (Winds of Abandon, Cataclysmic Gearhulk) that fit a plan. Combos and infinite loops are foundational MagicΓÇöplay removal instead of capping them. Fetchlands/duals shouldn't be banned; they add texture despite being band-aids on Magic's mana base. Avoid excessive tap lands and MDFCs that force off-curve plays.

## Key Clips

- Seated sealed pre-release: "you have one pack that's seated towards only one of the colors." When your seeded pack's colors match your opened rares and mythics, the deck builds itself and is way stronger; when they mismatch, you get an awkward, weaker pool.
- On attacking players with no blockers: "if someone doesn't have blockers and you're a deck that wants to go to combat, just hit them." EDH life totals are so high that hitting an open player for free is a public service, and an empty board often signals a player about to combo off, not a helpless one.
- Read missed land drops as a threat: when an opponent keeps deploying one- and two-drops but misses land drops, "those are spells and once those spells come online how messed up are you going to be"ΓÇöthey likely kept a powerful synergy hand short on lands.
- Partner critique: balance problems come from "probably like seven or eight specific designs" (Thrasios, Tymna, Dargo, Rograkh), not the mechanic itself. Keeping partners mono-color and below normal commander power level (e.g. Livio) makes them safe; two-color partners are the danger.
- Bracket 3 deckbuilding insight: "what you shouldn't be looking at is how many game changers your deck needs, but how quickly you can deploy interaction for faster decks." In bracket 2 you have many turns before needing removal; in bracket 3 that need comes much earlier, so pack enchantment removal for turn-three Necropotence.
- Board wipe philosophy: people slot in the two best wraths (Farewell, Day of Judgment) to fill a quota instead of choosing synergistic or asymmetric wipes. Mass exile like Farewell is bad for the game; one-sided or partial wipes (Winds of Abandon, Cataclysmic Gearhulk, Wave Goodbye) keep you developing instead of resetting to parityΓÇöand Farewell in an aggro deck like Adeline is self-defeating.
- On "infinite combos should be capped": combos are "a foundational part of Magic"ΓÇöevery format has them. Capping loops opens a can of worms (do I get 10 more on each turn? does swapping a sac outlet reset the count?). The real answer to combos is interaction: "Play removal. That is the answer to every single question."
- On banning duals/fetches: fetchlands are too ingrained to remove, and removing the best lands just makes shocks/triomes the new bestΓÇöeverything still gets compared to a top option. Fetches add texture (surveil, triome fixing, graveyard count, landfall doubling, life payment) and should just be cheaper; proxying duals is more defensible than proxying fetches for game pace.
