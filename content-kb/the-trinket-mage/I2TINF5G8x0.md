## Summary

This Magic Mirror podcast episode reacts to listener hot takes after the hosts' Secrets of Strixhaven prerelease (a seated Sealed event they found luck-heavy but fun, mostly Prismari pools). Key strategic points: attack open players because EDH life totals are high and a creatureless or mana-light opponent is often hiding threats or about to combo, not helpless. Stax suffers from Wizards under-supporting it, so only egregious pieces see play. Partner is a dangerous keyword (a free extra card); narrow, low-power partners like Tormod the Desecrator are fine, while two-color partners (Thrasios, Tymna) are overpowered—more niche designs are welcome but two-color partners won't return. Bracket 3 is hard to gauge; build for fast interaction, not game-changer count. Lightning Greaves is an average staple, over-included (~25% of decks) and often a 'kick me' sign inviting removal. Board wipes are over-jammed thoughtlessly; one-sided, incomplete, or scalable wipes (Cataclysmic Gearhulk, Winds of Abandon, By Invitation Only) reward play skill. Capping combos is anti-Magic; answer combos with removal and rule-zero. Fetchlands and dual lands shouldn't be banned—fetches add gameplay texture and decisions—but should be cheaper. Run only a few MDFC and tapped lands; playing off-curve repeatedly costs games.

## Key Clips

- **[09:24]** If someone doesn't have blockers and you're a deck that wants to go to combat, just hit them. People play a 5/5 or 6/6 commander and never attack with it — if your opponent has no creatures, you should probably be hitting them.
- **[10:54]** If you see somebody struggling really hard for lands, they probably kept an opening hand with powerful synergy pieces that just didn't have enough lands. Those missed land drops are spells — once they come online you can be in serious trouble, so attacking the land-light player is often correct.
- **[24:16]** If you're aiming for bracket 3, what you shouldn't be looking at is how many game changers your deck needs, but how quickly you can deploy interaction against faster decks. In bracket 2 you have many turns before you must play removal; in bracket 3 that comes much earlier, and people lose to turn-three Necropotence because they aren't packing enchantment removal.
- **[36:36]** Don't just jam the two best wraths to fill a board-wipe quota. Think about your deck's play pattern — if you're the go-wide aggro deck like Adeline, you're the one opponents want to wipe, so Farewell is wrong for you. Consider one-sided or incomplete wipes like Winds of Abandon, Cataclysmic Gearhulk, or damage-based sweepers that let you break parity instead of resetting the game.
- **[42:24]** Combos are a foundational part of Magic — every format has them. The answer to combos isn't banning the archetype; it's play removal. If a combo deck is equal power level to the rest of the table, it shouldn't be a problem; the issue is usually mismatched pace.
- **[44:51]** When deck building, most people only plan their A-to-B strategy and never ask: what happens if someone plays a board wipe, makes more tokens than me, or assembles a combo? Build realistic answers to those scenarios, because when you have no answer to a combo the game simply ends.
- **[57:02]** Basic lands are really good. If you find yourself playing off curve more than once a game because of too many tap lands, you're behind — doing it two or three times in a game adds up and is actually pretty bad. Don't max out on utility tap lands at the expense of untapped basics.
- **[54:46]** On how many MDFC lands to run: average about four or five a deck. The two-color ones are great, but most should function as tap lands unless they're weak; the strongest 'bolt'-style MDFCs feel almost obligatory, which is a sign they're a little too pushed.
