## Summary

Corser of Cruix functions as a draw engine and source of card advantage. It digs deeper into your library by removing lands from the top, and it creates advantage by letting you play those lands directly from library rather than from hand. A common objection — that playing lands off the top while lands accumulate in hand isn't true card advantage — is rejected: any land played off the top can be imagined as having gone to hand, with a hand-land played instead, so the lands piling up in hand would equally be present had you simply drawn the cards. They may not be the identical lands, which is the only real cost. For decks that strongly depend on which specific lands hit land drops four through seven, that substitution introduces a minor downside. But for roughly 95% of decks, that distinction doesn't matter, and the card is straightforwardly a draw spell worth treating as card advantage.

## Key Clips

- Would you consider Corser of Cruix a draw spell? This one's an unambiguous yes from me. It digs deeper into your library by getting lands out of the way, and it creates card advantage by letting you play lands from library instead of from your hand. That's a draw engine.
- You might think, is it really card advantage to play lands off the top and then let lands pile up in your hand instead? And yes, yes, it is.
- Anytime you play a land off the top, imagine instead that it went to your hand and you played a land from your hand instead. Those lands piling up in hand would be there if you drew the cards instead. They just might not be the exact same lands.
- If your deck strongly cares about what exact lands get played for land drops four, five, six, and seven, maybe there's a downside here. But for the other 95% of decks, this card is just plain good.
