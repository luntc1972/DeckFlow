## Summary

The video argues against reflexively upgrading every Commander/EDH deck past a healthy mid-power level. Three reasons not to upgrade: (1) Decks with structural bottlenecksΓÇöreliance on a 3+ mana commander's board presence, graveyard buildup, board wipes, or goadΓÇöstay vulnerable to hate (command-zone removal, graveyard exile) that card-quality upgrades can't fix; example: the Sharuum Slide deck. (2) "Cheap decks" like Feather scale logarithmicallyΓÇöyou already run the best 1-2 mana instants/sorceries, so further spend means swapping core pieces for generic value cards, weakening the gameplan (also the Meria equipment deck). (3) You may dislike the resulting power level, where games end via turn-4/5 combos or relentless hate, forcing combos in that don't suit the deck (his Glissa battlecruiser deck stays mid). Power level comes from card quality plus cohesion; players overfocus on card quality since it's a clean money-for-power trade, while cohesion is hard to retune. Upgradability hinges on whether a deck has a strong, flexible core converting cards into generic resourcesΓÇöYuriko (2-mana ninjutsu, fast/resilient/simple) upgrades well; Feather doesn't. Mid-power decks are valid; know the cost before pouring money in, or build a new home for a card/theme you love.

## Key Clips

- There are actually some pretty compelling reasons not to upgrade a deck further once it plays smoothly at a healthy middling power level.
- The first reason not to upgrade a deck is that some decks have natural bottlenecks in their functionalityΓÇöbeing vulnerable to a particular type of hate or having a game plan that is fundamentally difficult to speed up. If your deck relies heavily on the board presence of a commander that's more than three mana, opponents only need to hate out your command zone a couple times early to shut you down.
- An extension on this problem is 'the cheap deck'ΓÇöa deck that works well at low budgets but is very logarithmic in how it translates additional budget to power. Feather is the best example: 1-2 mana instants and sorceries that target creatures are cheap, so you're already running the best ones, and the only way to improve is cutting them for generic value cards, which weakens the Feather game plan.
- The other reason not to increase a deck's power level is if you simply don't enjoy the power level you end up at. You might end up facing decks that combo off on turn 5 or voltron kill on turn 4, and you need to ask whether that's the sort of MTG you want to be playing.
- There are two main factors that determine a deck's power level: card qualityΓÇöhow good the cards are, and cohesionΓÇöhow well the cards are put together. We tend to overwhelmingly focus on the first when thinking about upgrading, because it's an unambiguous exchange of money for power, whereas cohesion is much harder to modulate on an already-built deck.
- Yuriko, the Tiger's Shadow doesn't have the same ceiling as Feather because she's fundamentally faster, more resilient, and simpler. Commander ninjutsu at two mana converts low-cost ninjas and evasive creatures into very fundamental resourcesΓÇöcard advantage and life lossΓÇöwith minimal mana investment. This strong core for generating generic resources is what makes a deck especially upgradable.
- There's nothing wrong with commanders better suited to mid-power, nor is a commander like Yuriko or Korvold that pushes higher power inherently more interesting. Some players like the challenge of boosting a theme that isn't inherently high power, and that can be a fun process.
- If you have a mid-power deck you really like, you don't necessarily need to change it. If you do want a higher power deck, you can pick out a couple things you like from an existing deck you enjoyΓÇöa card or a themeΓÇöand then build a new, more powerful home for it.
