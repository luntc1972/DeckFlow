## Summary

This video reframes Stax in EDH. Stax means resource denial: cards restrict the table's resources, then break parity. The host argues mass land destruction (Armageddon) is taboo while board wipes (Wrath) are accepted purely for social, not objective, reasonsΓÇöplayers prepare for wipes but not land hate. He critiques how 'player agency' is invoked unevenly: hard stax draws side-eye while snowball midrange, Teferi's Protection, The One Ring, and Cyclonic Rift escape scrutiny despite limiting opponents' meaningful actions. He distinguishes primary resources (mana, cardsΓÇöneeded to play) from secondary resources (needed to win), recasting stax as proactive (attacks primary resources) and control as reactive (attacks secondary). In multiplayer EDH, control and stax blur because all decks run ramp/draw and hating mana becomes necessary. His verdict: hard stax suits cEDH and high power, but at mid-power it just makes opponents 'putter around' miserably. Better solutions: incomplete board wipes (Cataclysmic Gearhulk, Urza's Sylex), soft hate (lands enter tapped, shuffle-into-library graveyard hate), tax and rule-of-law effects, and slug/life-loss stax (Liesa) that pressure without removing agency. He wants Wizards to print more soft hate to lower the format's battlecruiser height while preserving interaction.

## Key Clips

- Mass land destruction is acutely painful largely because people don't prepare for it, and people don't prepare for it because it's not commonly accepted as a major part of the format. The reason why people are frequently prepared for one and frequently unprepared for the other comes down to social factors.
- The most basic definition of stax is that it's resource denial. Broadly, a stax deck is one that looks to restrict the table's resources, and then break parity on those restrictionsΓÇöbasically to do something to everybody, and then play in a way that isn't as affected by that thing.
- Stax is proactive because it attacks primary resources, the basic building blocks required for playing the game, whereas control is reactive because it attacks secondary resources, the ones required for winning. Secondary resources are what is directly required to win games, and primary resources are what is required to get thereΓÇöcards and mana.
- Being a control deck in EDH pretty much always means hating out opposing sources of cards and manaΓÇöthat's just how the game is played. It's near-impossible to fully stabilize if a bunch of faster decks are able to keep their value engines, and powerful mana enhancers can likewise pose a problem.
- In cEDH and the highest reaches of high power casual, these things are absolutely good tools. So yes, high-caliber hate in all forms will be useful in the highest powers of commander, and will have a positive effect in contesting the most powerful proactive decks. However, at the more middling power levels that most players play at, these cards don't constitute such an unambiguous good.
- This idea of an incomplete boardwipe is also a great solution for landsΓÇöKeldon Firebombers and Urza's Sylex are examples of land hate that is less severe than other land hate specifically because it allows players to keep around lands. Keeping even three lands is so much less punishing than keeping around none at all.
- Slug stax cards subvert the agency critique by forcing players to pay with a resource that is, by default, quite plentiful in commanderΓÇölife total. This means that stax decks, traditionally a more controlling strategy, can take on new life as a beatdown strategy, forcing opponents to make hard choices about whether to do the plays they want to and face life total consequences.
- This format will be at its most vibrant when it can find a balance between player agency and interactivity between players, where players can do their own thing but where that thing might need to take into account the actions of opponents. Players should feel pressured by what opponents are doing, but they shouldn't necessarily feel oppressed.
