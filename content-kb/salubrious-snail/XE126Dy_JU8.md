## Summary

The video defends Eminence, the keyword (on five legendary commanders plus an unofficial sixth) that lets one line of a commander's text work from the command zone, with a stronger follow-up effect once cast. Its bad reputation comes from specific design mistakes, not the mechanic itself. Edgar Markov and The Ur-Dragon became the two most popular commanders yet are problematic: both are five-color soups attached to iconic tribes (vampires, dragons), crowding out other commanders, commanding triple-digit prices, and granting power without even casting the card. The author proposes design rules: keep Eminence commanders to two colors or fewer when they risk overshadowing alternatives; match payoff power to trigger specificity (easy trigger needs specific payoff, like Oloro/Inalla; generic payoff needs preventable trigger, like Arahbo); never directly grant cards or mana (only indirectly, with an extra step); and make the creature genuinely worth casting (Edgar and Inalla fail this). Arahbo's interactable, specific cat-pump is praised. The best use case is aggro, since Eminence raises a glass-cannon deck's early floor while the creature compounds the advantage later. Example custom designs (equipment counters, conditional noncombat damage, goad, downside abilities) show the space. Eminence should return, used with care and restraint.

## Key Clips

- Eminence is a keyword officially on five legendary creatures and unofficially on a sixth, and its effect is simple—it lets one line of text on a commander also work while the card is in the command zone.
- In May, Atraxa Praetors' Voice lost her position as the most popular commander. Then, last month, she lost her position as the second most popular commander as well. What replaced her was The Ur Dragon and Edgar Markov, both with the keyword 'eminence'.
- Both of these commanders ended up being by far the strongest option for their creature type, and that fact then pushed any new Dragon or Vampire player looking for a shortcut to a strong deck to get there by paying a lot of money for the singular strongest commander. Edgar Markov spent over four years above the $50 mark and the Ur Dragon spent two of those years at a similar price.
- The difficulty and specificity of the setup needs to match the power of the payoff. If triggering an eminence ability is easy, the payoff should be less generic and require more ingenuity to take advantage of, as is the case with Oloro, and to a lesser extent Inalla. If an eminence ability has a stronger and more generic payoff, it should have a triggering condition that is more specific or more preventable, as is true of Arahbo.
- Gavin Verhey, who worked on the design for Edgar Markov and other eminence cards, has said that future Eminence designs should avoid generating permanent on-board material for players.
- The design rule I'd set here is simple—eminence abilities should not directly give cards or mana. Indirectly is fine—all of the eminence commander abilities are frequently used to generate cards, mana, or both in their respective decks, but there needs to be an extra step involved to increase the specificity and interactivity of this sort of ability.
- Aggro is actually challenging on a structural level with regard to commanders themselves. There are a bunch of fast deck styles that are only propped up by the presence of a somewhat fragile commander, leading to glass cannon decks that are capable of crushing players but fall apart to two removal spells. What eminence does, then, is allow you to split the difference, with the eminence effect boosting a deck's early game and overall floor, and the commander creature offering a way to compound that advantage later in the game.
- Eminence is a mechanic that is challenging to implement well, and which carries the full possibility to easily veer off course and into problems. But importantly, that's only because the mechanic poses such a fundamental shakeup to the structure of the command zone, and that feature of it also imbues it with a ton of rich gameplay possibilities.
