## Summary

The creator traces a decade-long quest to capture one vibe: a greedy 'fat control' battlecruiser deck that builds huge mana and card advantage, becomes the table's archenemy, and crushes opponents through sheer volume of gameplay. His original mono-black Sheoldred deck (2014) was a removal pile—10+ board wipes, 18 removal spells, fragile bursty wincons like Exsanguinate, too high-curved, too few lands. The Glissa Sunslayer deck fixed structural flaws by adding green for land-based ramp (sticky, removal-resistant) and diverse removal across all card types. Key build principles: pick commander first, build the 99 around her defense and draw; ramp on a low curve to play Glissa turn 2; fold wincons into the ramp/draw/removal suites so support cards ARE the win plan (19 top-end threats with removal/ramp/draw attached); skip non-land tutors to preserve consistency and reduce decision anxiety in a comfort deck. But Glissa stayed too oppressive (24 interaction pieces) and too generic (best-in-class staples felt pubstompy). His Radha, Heir to Keld deck solves both: fewer interaction spells (16) with deliberate gaps forcing aggressive play, plus a budget (~$75), no-cards-under-4-mana gimmick, and cascade for unique identity.

## Key Clips

- **[03:08]** Its ramp suite was entirely rocks, doublers, and Cabal-coffers-type effects, stuff that works well when I'm being ignored but which can be snuffed out with a couple removal spells. This problem can be solved by simply adding green—I want a lot of mana, and the way to get a lot of mana and have it stick around is to get a lot of lands.
- **[03:57]** The deck felt disjointed in its win plan. It had some powerful win outlets, but they were both explosive and fragile, which reduced the fun factor of the deck... So, I omitted basically all bursty wincons from the Glissa deck. Instead, the deck will be winning through big creatures, or with noncreature wincons that require a gamestate where I'm far ahead.
- **[05:49]** A key component in building around Glissa was mana curve—most games, the deck wants to play Glissa on curve, which means starting with a bunch of one-cost ramp, allowing me to play Glissa on turn 2.
- **[07:14]** That's the secret sauce—the deck's wincons are built into its ramp, draw, and removal suites. It runs 19 of what I'll call top end threats, and of these, 10 have some sort of removal attached, 3 have some sort of ramp attached, and 3 have some sort of draw attached... since the support cards are the wincon, the deck gets to have its cake and eat it too.
- **[08:10]** Tutors aren't even that necessary—draw enough cards, and you'll find what you need. The second reason is that this is a comfort deck, and something that's equal parts compelling and anxiety-inducing about tutors is that they offer possibilities. Tutoring the correct card for a given situation can be difficult.
- **[10:00]** This deck was simply too honed-in on and effective at crushing the rest of the table to be any sort of default, comfort deck... The other issue is that it was too generic. The commander was a generically good card draw engine, and it ran a whole lot of best-in-class top end cards and removal spells. The result was that the deck didn't really feel unique.
- **[11:51]** A lot of players try to avoid not being able to deal with a particular thing, to be perfectly equipped for every potential threat, but I've actually quite enjoyed the challenge this incomplete removal suite poses... Forcing the deck to get aggressive to deal with some types of threats adds a lot of texture to the deck.
- **[13:24]** In truth the budget aspect of this deck is a big part of what makes it feel good. If I was raining down grade A bombs like Etali and Ancient Copper Dragon and Old Gnawbone and Kozilek on my opponents, a lot of the scrappy feeling of this deck would be lost. Winning with these terrifying threats that everyone has seen before has a very different vibe than winning with Screamer Killer, Flameblast Dragon.
