## Summary

This video argues lands deserve the same deckbuilding intention as nonland cards, pushing back on the mindset that lands are a necessary evil to minimize. Frank Karsten's land-count formula (built on 60-card data) is a good start, but his EDH-specific simulation—optimizing for curve-out against a passive opponent—better fits Commander, recommending roughly 37 to 42 lands. Commanders that draw or filter cheaply (Rona, Yasharn) justify fewer lands; commanders costing 4+ that draw cards, or decks heavy on expensive draw, justify running more lands, since big draw cuts through extra lands and reduces screw. Many players run too few lands out of attachment to nonland cards rather than testing. Color count and symbol density also matter: without expensive fetches and shocks, you must run fewer colors or more lands. A four-color manabase example shows budget and tapland tradeoffs, where bumping 30 lands to 40 restores symbol access. As shorthand, roughly 30 symbols gives great access to demanding cards, 25 good for double-symbol, 20 mediocre, 15 unreliable. Apply Karsten's strict 89+M% standard to pivotal on-curve cards; later cards tolerate fewer symbols if draw and ramp support them. Treat the manabase as part of your deck.

## Key Clips

- A lot of people don't really see lands as a part of their deck in any meaningful sense. If your hand was missing something else you find vital to your gameplan—an artifact, a creature, a piece of ramp—you wouldn't say 'wow, this opening hand would be great if it had an artifact in it,' because that's a silly statement to make.
- Frank Karsten's format-agnostic land-count analysis takes match data across a couple years of online MTG events, excludes any deck that didn't have more wins than losses, then runs a regression analysis asking how mana curve, quantity of cheap draw spells, and quantity of cheap ramp spells affect how many lands a successful deck plays. The result is turned into a formula to determine the number of lands a given deck should play.
- Karsten's EDH-specific article obtained its findings through simulated games against a passive opponent, optimizing for the highest quality curve-out. It factors in commander-specific assumptions like the free mulligan and the commander being effectively an extra nonland card available at all times. It concludes that for purposes of curving out properly, decks would be well served to run at least 37 lands, and as many as 42 in some cases.
- If your commander costs 4 or more and draws cards, not only should you not run less lands than you otherwise would—you should run more. That's a draw spell you'll have every single game, and your only barrier between yourself and those sweet cards is drawing into enough lands or ramp to get there. Medium-to-large draw will cut through a slightly higher density of lands with no problem.
- If you can justify buying fetches and shocks, or if you feel like proxying, you should run them. If you're like me and you can't justify that purchase, you should either play fewer colors or run more lands. Dialing land count up from 30 to 40 with equivalent per-card budget jumped symbol counts back up to 20 for core colors and 13 for off-colors, matching the more tapland-oriented 30 land option.
- As shorthand: 30 symbols in your mana base gives great access to all but the most symbol-intensive cards, 25 symbols gives great access to single symbol cards and decent access to double symbol cards, 20 gives decent access to single symbol cards and mediocre access to more, and 15 symbols gives okay access to single symbol cards but is unreliable beyond that.
- Apply the strict 89+M% standard for pivotal cards you want to land on curve, such as a commander or a ramp spell. For stuff you need a little later—high drops, combo pieces, or removal—you can put them behind a substantially lower symbol count as long as you've got enough draw and ramp to help you get the needed symbols by the time you need those cards.
- My Glissa deck runs 41 total lands with 31 green sources and 22 black sources. It's justifiable: a high-curved deck, card draw in the command zone, ideal curve of elf on one and commander on 2. Mana issues the first three turns would dramatically slow the deck since it can't get its draw and ramp online. Mana screw is optional—if you run a deck with big draw, running more lands is your way of unticking that box.
