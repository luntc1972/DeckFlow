## Summary

This video argues that staying in a fixed EDH playgroup holds players back, and that leaving it improves deck-building and enjoyment of Commander. Regular playgroups offer balance, familiarity with friends' decks, and house rules, but create a bubble: if no one plays artifacts, combos, graveyard, or creatureless strategies, you gain no experience against them and become a worse player. This narrowness causes culture shock at game stores, where unfamiliar combo, control, polymorph, or reanimator decks blindside you, and where one-game wins feel like pub stomps because you never see a stranger's average power level. Playgroups also become 'inbred,' tuning decks to a local meta (e.g., anti-blue cards) that are dead against random opponents. Playing randos teaches new cards, commanders, and brackets; the host left a bracket-four group, forced himself to build slower bracket-two decks, and rediscovered low-power EDH. The healthy goal isn't winning every game but improving knowledge so losses come from wacky moments, not misplays. Broadening your meta means adding graveyard hate and instant-speed interaction to be ready for anything. To leave your playgroup, try a local game store Commander night, larger events like MagicCon, or Spell Table for webcam games.

## Key Clips

- Your playgroup is holding you back. I know mine did. Look, I love my friends and my original playgroups, but I think leaving them has done a lot to improve my deck-building, skill level, and opinion of EDH overall.
- If the only decks you ever see are the same ones your friends play, then that becomes the only way you interact with the format. So if nobody in your playgroup has an artifact deck, you will simply have no experience with those. If no one in your playgroup uses combos, graveyard decks, or creatureless strategies, then your view of magic is going to be very skewed.
- It's easier to learn about a deck by meeting a player who has that list and asking them, "Do you like the deck? What are the weaknesses? What are the strengths?" Most players like their deck and are willing to yap about them. And if you find that that mill deck is very frustrating to deal with, simply asking them, "How do you lose games?" will be a good way to point you in the right direction of how to beat this deck.
- We forget that with strangers, we're only seeing one game, probably. And with friends, we see multiple games and can actually tell what the average power level of their deck is, or how their deck works. The benefit of the doubt doesn't seem to extend to randos. But if you take this idea and believe that any win can be had, assuming you played better or constructed your deck better, then you can always improve in casual EDH.
- When I say improve, I don't mean strictly improve. It's improving for the meta game of playing against random players as opposed to your play group. In my casual decks, I don't have any storm hate cards because the archetype is almost never played in casual Commander. But if your local meta or friend group has someone who plays storm all the time, then maybe you should play some anti-storm cards.
- My old play group was basically all bracket four all the time. Every deck would have to be ready for MLD or fast combos. After graduating school and leaving that bracket four play group, I forced myself to build slower decks for playing at random game stores. This was not only a deck building challenge that I think led to some of my most interesting decks, but it also made me much better at playing and deck building.
- Where should you go to leave your playgroup? The easy answer is to find a local game store that has a Commander night. You can also go to larger events like a Magic Con. Lastly, if you don't have a game store near you, then Spell Table is another place to go. It lets you play games with people over the internet via webcam. It's cheap, you just need a webcam, but the anonymity of the internet does make the average Spell Table user less nice than they might otherwise act if it was in person.
