## Summary

This video argues against treating ramp spells like Cultivate as universal must-plays in EDH, prompted by 60% of Toski and Tovolar decks running it on EDHRec. The creator says EDH is especially prone to over-hyping staples because four players, singleton, and 100-card decks leave room for fluff and slow feedback. Cultivate (and Kodama's Reach) trades speed for an extra land to hand, which suits slow, multi-color decks needing color fixing and a pivotal 5-6 mana turn—but is wasteful in mono-color Toski (33 forests already fix mana) or two-color Tovolar with low top-end. Key selection factors: mana curve (build ramp toward the value the deck actually wants, e.g. four), card type (dorks/rocks die to removal but are cheaper; land-grab spells dodge land destruction), and net vs upfront mana cost (untapped-land grabbers like Skyshroud Claim, Harrow, Grow from the Ashes give flexibility; signets and Mind Stone depend on timing). Examples: Glissa runs dorks, Meria runs rocks, a Scapeshift deck favors land-grabbers. How much ramp to run depends on playtesting feel. The broader point: scrutinize the unglamorous parts—lands, removal, ramp—because they let the deck's core run smoothly.

- On both pages, 60% of decks run Cultivate. Now, obviously EDHRec has some inherent flaws that get further compounded through usage of the website. With a commander that's low-mana enough not to be boosted out by the ramp part and who draws enough cards to not need the tacked on land-to-hand. You could spend that three mana on literally anything else.
- There will be the cards that truly should be added to most decks on the basis of power—Sol Ring, Counterspell, Mystic Remora—but there will also be cards regarded in the same lens but which truly don't belong in all or even most decks, and I want to focus on a specific category of cards among these: mana ramp spells.
- Cultivate, along with its twin Kodama's Reach, is a Rampant Growth that costs an extra mana and has a Lay of the Land attached. When you play Cultivate, you're actively choosing an extra land to hand rather than the speed of a two mana ramp spell, and this is absolutely a tradeoff that some decks want. The more colors you play, the more that extra land will matter.
- As a general principle Cultivate is best geared toward decks looking for a pivotal 5 or 6 mana turn. This brings me to a major consideration when picking out ramp—mana curve. A lot of decks will have one or more major mana value numbers they care about, and your ramp package should be formulated to fit this. When you're adding a ramp spell to a deck, you should be asking 'what is this ramping toward?'
- Card type is another consideration with regard to ramp, since artifacts and especially creatures will die to removal and board wipes, but land destruction is much less common. However, dorks and rocks are often more efficiently costed than cards that grab lands, so there are tradeoffs to be had. Single green dorks tend to be the most mana-efficient ramp option at lower budgets.
- A final consideration when considering ramp cards is the net mana cost vs the upfront mana cost. For example, Skyshroud Claim has an upfront cost of 4, but a net cost of 2 if you grab Forests that enter untapped. Two ramp spells I generally prefer to Cultivate in 2 color decks are Harrow and Grow from the Ashes, as they grab untapped lands and thereby allow for more flexible turns than a card that grabs tapped lands.
- The best way to tell the right amount of ramp for your deck is, well, to play it and see if it feels like it has the right amount of ramp. If you're ramping to a particular value, does the deck succeed at that consistently? If you find yourself drawing too many ramp spells but want the same ramping capability, you can cut some small ones for a bigger one or two.
- I think when it comes to the traditionally simpler parts of an EDH deck—the lands, the removal, the ramp—a lot of people have the idea that they can kinda gloss over these things and focus their efforts on the juicy synergies. But these parts are what lets the core of the deck do its thing smoothly, so taking a critical eye toward them will likely improve your deck more than you think.
