## Summary

This video lays out a conceptual framework for aggro decks in Commander/EDH. The core problem: EDH's 40-life format defeats traditional 20-life aggro mathΓÇö39 Lightning Bolts or many Delver swings are needed, so brute-force damage doesn't work. The author introduces "Aggro Ideology": because 40 life makes lifepay cheap, blocking optional, and snowbally setup turns common, the real goal isn't reducing opponents to zero but pressuring their life totals (mantra: keep threats at 15 life) so they must play conservatively. The second key feature is commander damageΓÇö21 damage from a commander effectively turns it into a 21-life format. These combine into a diagram of viable aggro types: pure voltron (buff/protect commander for fast kills), pseudo-voltron (commander damage plus other damage, multiple win plans), and disruptive aggro (multi-opponent damage paired with stax). Practical challenges discussed: connecting with life totals via evasion or removing blockers; surviving boardwipes through speed, permanent protection (Selfless Spirit), or Blood Artist effects; balancing speed against resilience; and target prioritization. The author notes aggro is sometimes seen as rude at fiddly midrange tables but defends it as legitimate variety in a social format.

## Key Clips

- What about in commander though? That 120 health format we all know and love. A mere 39 lightning bolts are needed, presuming each player gutshots themself with a land. Or 39 swings with a Delver of Secrets. The answer is that it doesn't work. The fact that EDH is a 40 life format is a pain point for our hypothetical red-deck-winner.
- First of all, lifepay is so much less punishing. Fetches and shocks? Easy. Phyrexian mana symbols? Close enough to free. Paying 1 life for 1 card? An even more delicious bargain than usual. Secondly, blocking isn't as necessary. Decks are empowered to constantly use tap abilities, to swing in for value with a Bident of Thassa effect, and to eat 5-10 damage hits rather than blocking with an important creature.
- My mantra in a game where somebody has a deck that is concerning and unpredictable but not necessarily totally dominant, is that they should be at 15 life. When somebody is at 15 life, they have to play much more conservatively. Having safe setup turns isn't a given, and they're generally one round of attacks away from death if they start doing concerning things. Going forward, I'm going to refer to this concept as Aggro Ideology.
- If your commander deals 21 combat damage to an opponent, they lose. This means that if your deck is commander based, you're playing a 21 health format instead of the much more challenging 40 health format.
- On the right here, we have pure voltron. This is a deck that plays a commander, buffs it and protects it through a combination of other cards, and kills players very quickly in large bursts of damage. In the middle here is pseudo-voltron. These decks will commonly utilize commander damage to win, but will often have other forms of damage as well. The last type of deck here is disruptive aggro. This is a deck that fully commits to the plan of making its opponents uncomfortable. You'll commonly see lots of cards that damage multiple opponents, often paired with some form of stax.
- Maintaining creatures on board is a separate challenge, with the roadblock there usually being boardwipes. For one, be faster than the deck with the boardwipes, and target them first. Alternatively run protection spellsΓÇöpermanent-based is preferable for an aggro deck, with Selfless Spirit being the absolute gold standard if you're running white. As a third option, run Blood Artist effectsΓÇöthis is my preferred strategy when playing black, as it ties in beautifully with our Aggro Ideology.
- Past the basics, a key skill you'll want to develop is target prioritization. If your deck prefers to kill one player at a time, you need to pick a player to focus, and picking wrong might lose you the game. Primary targets that come to mind are players who pay a lot of life, and players who have especially strong ways to shut you down, but it's definitely a vibes-based thing and when in doubt you can just attack the opponent you're most afraid of.
- A final challenge I'll mention is that at some tables, aggro is considered rude in some capacity. This is especially common in pods populated with fiddly midrange decks, where knocking a player out early means they'll spend upwards of an hour sitting on the sidelines.
