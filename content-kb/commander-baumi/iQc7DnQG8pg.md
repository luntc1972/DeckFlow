## Summary

This video documents the creator's self-designed casual Commander event run Friday nights at Dex Dice and Dungeons in Gizen, Germany, built to fix problems with typical LGS formats: tournament structures that punish casual play, and unstructured free play that creates power/personality mismatches and excludes new players. Four systems combine. First, timed rounds: two ~90-100 minute rounds with hard cutoffs, plus table generation via goodenoughgolfers.com (social golfer problem) so players face new opponents each round. Second, bracket matchmaking: players sign up for brackets 2, 3, and/or 4, and pods are paired by desired bracket, front-loading the rule-zero conversation. Third, a quest system drawn from 600+ custom quests sorted into generic, archetype, thematic, and wildcard pools; players complete five of six tasks (never 'win the game'), rewarding play rather than winning, and adaptable to holidays or set releases. Fourth, a flat raffle: players buy two boosters (keep one, donate one to the prize pool), earning raffle tickets per completed quest, so even losers can win. The event grew from a handful to 30+ players. The creator offers to share the spreadsheet system in exchange for feedback and plans to build an app.

## Key Clips

- There are two common kinds of LGS commander nights. The first is a tournament or tournament-adjacent structure, and the second is nothing — you just show up and find a table to play. This can work fine, but if you're unlucky, you might get stuck at a table with power or personality mismatches.
- Rule zero has a timing problem. Ideally players would first discuss their desired game experience and then find like-minded folks to sit down with. But instead players usually first find the table and then hope an agreement is possible. The safest option is to arrange pods with players you already know, but that creates smaller subgroups that make the community less accessible to new players.
- I use goodenoughgolfers.com to generate approximate solutions of the social golfer problem — matchmaking players across multiple rounds so they don't play the same opponents twice. I generate two rounds of pods, each running roughly 90 to 100 minutes. Since the cutoff point is known ahead of time, players adjust their gameplay to fit, and the vast majority of matches finish on time.
- When players sign up they can sign up for any variation of brackets 2, 3, and 4. For a matchmaking system to be robust it needs as few queues as possible — bracket 1 doesn't exist and bracket 5 is basically a different game, so they are natural exclusions. The matchmaking can't fully replace the rule zero discussion, but it frontloads a huge section of it so players at each table already share an idea of what they want.
- One of the fundamental issues with Commander is that the only structural reward the game can provide is winning. As a social game it struggles to reward players that don't win. Ideally there would be smaller incremental achievements a player can earn through a match, so that even if they lose they still feel like they accomplished something. That's what I like about quests.
- I have over 600 unique quests split into categories: generic quests any deck can do, archetype quests that need specific decks, thematic quests, and wildcard quests — the weird stuff I only want one of per sheet. Each sheet has six quests and players complete five. There are no 'win the game' quests; they're all things the players themselves can do.
- To participate, players buy two booster packs. One they keep, the other goes into the prize pool raffled off at the end. Every player starts with two raffle tickets and gains one per completed quest, up to seven, and can only win twice. The complete range of prizes is one to three packs, with everybody always getting one. It's a flat structure players can influence but not dominate.
- My favorite moment of feedback was when a friend told me he was happy to walk away a winner. I asked if he did well in his games and he said no, he lost them horribly, but he completed the quests and won a booster in the raffle, and to him that was enough of a victory to consider the whole evening positive. That's exactly what I want.
