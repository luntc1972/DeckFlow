## Summary

This video argues commander-centric decks built around kill-on-sight commanders can succeed with proper deckbuilding and gameplay. The core principle: never cast a kill-on-sight commander on curve and tapped out, because opponents will remove it. Instead, be patient and overramp significantlyΓÇöwait until you have six or seven mana for a four-mana commander so you have resources to protect it and to recast it if removed. Delaying also dodges the first (and ideally second) wave of removal and can force opponents into inefficiency, as shown by a Minn deck held back as a silver bullet. Treat commander abilities like ETBs: have rituals, card draw, or follow-up spells ready so casting immediately generates value opponents must answer too late. For deckbuilding: keep your curve functional without the commander rather than stacking all spells off-commander CMC; minimize cards that only work with the commander in play (prefer creature-based buffers like Guardian Augment over Giant Growth on Sergeant John Benton); and run backup redundancy (e.g., Tef'fair's Ageless Insight backing up Vinovix). Excess ramp underpins every step.

## Key Clips

- I disagree with the idea that commander-centric decks are doomed to fail. There are certain steps we need to take to make sure they function. This includes deck building, but also making good gameplay decisions.
- The first, and really the worst trap, is when they just play their commander on curve, tapped out. Don't play a kill-on-sight commander without any backup. We want to wait with casting our commander until we are ready to defend it, and that often means not playing it on curve.
- Our best friend is ramp. And I don't mean to ramp out the commander quickly. Rather, we should try to overramp significantly. If we're playing a four mana commander, it's often a good idea to wait until we have six or seven mana available before we cast them. That way we have resources available to defend them, and we probably already dodged the first wave of removal.
- By preserving his commander and holding it as a silver bullet for the right moment, he was able to exert a much higher degree of pressure that was incredibly difficult to deal with. Delaying our commander can force our opponents into a difficult position of inefficiency.
- The scenario we want to avoid is our commander getting removed and then us being forced to take a turn off because we don't have the mana to cast it again. If we ramped ahead of time, we will have the resources needed to stay in the match.
- Be ready to treat their abilities like they are an ETB. We want to maneuver into a position where when we cast our commander, we can immediately use them. We don't want to make the value we can get dependent on our opponents. Make it so that even if they get the kill, it's already kind of too late.
- The most common pitfall is lists that have their entire curve structured around their commander. A deck list like that is going to force us to play the commander on curve. It's better to keep the curve functional even without the commander. Make sure your supporting pieces can fill a curve all by themselves.
- You should be minimizing the cards that only do something with your commander in play. With Sergeant John Benton, giant growth doesn't do anything without our commander. Creature-based buffers like Guardian Augment or cards with alternative casting options like Light of Hope perform better. Finally, try to find backups for your commander for redundancy.
