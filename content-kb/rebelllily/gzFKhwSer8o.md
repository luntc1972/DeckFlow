## Summary

The video introduces Escalation Theory, a framework the creator developed (alongside their earlier Window Theory) as part of an RC application asking how to improve Commander. It plots every Commander game on a curve of timing (turns) versus effective value, where value is measured abstractly by permanents on board plus virtual value from card draw, ramp, and removal. Playing only lands yields a steady curve; mana dorks, rocks, and scaling engines bend it upward, while "mana's worth of effect" distinguishes board-advancing plays from cantrips that don't escalate. The core claim: friction in Commander isn't about power level or fairness, but about rhythmΓÇöwhether everyone's curve aligns. cEDH compresses the same curve into a tight, fast, sudden finish; casual battlecruiser games ramp and swing big. Cards like Dockside, Thassa's Oracle, and Coalition Victory feel salty when they skip or end the arc off-rhythm relative to the table's expectations. The creator frames this not as a ban argument but as a lens for evaluating negative play experiences. For deckbuilding, ask whether each card is merely additive or helps you scale; in play, use the curve to gauge opponents' trajectories and decide who and how to interact.

## Key Clips

- It's called escalation theory. We assume commander games are supposed to build up. Start small, ramp, deploy bigger threats, and eventually reach a payoff. But what happens when someone skips the build and ends the game early?
- Escalation Theory says every commander game follows a curve. The curve is made on an axis across timing in turns and effective value, which is measured in a very general and abstract way. Value is most easily determined by permanents on board, creatures, enchantments, artifacts, and so forth.
- The phrasing mana's worth of effect is also important to acknowledge because some decks set themselves up with lots of mana to take one really big turn, but the cards they've played in the past few turns may not have significantly improved their board like playing cantrips or loot effects.
- The key insight is this. It's not which curve you follow. It's whether the table is on the same one. For cEDH, everyone's curve is fast, tight, and ends suddenly. And it's great if everyone signed up for that.
- For casual battle cruiser, everyone ramps, builds engines, and swings big. The fun is in the buildup, but drop a combo in there and people feel cheated. Not because it's unfair, but because it's off rhythm.
- For Thassa's Oracle, it skips the arc completely. It ends the game early if the deck was designed to use it that way. And for a coalition victory, while they may seem fair and underpowered, they follow the curve and end it in an unsatisfying manner.
- Friction in commander often isn't about what happened. It's about when it happened relative to the table's curve. So this also explains that people keep hands to scale, not to stop each other.
- In play, escalation theory helps you visualize in an abstract way how much value your opponents are developing in their curve. It helps you imagine where they are going, who is ahead or behind, and how far they could get capped by the right piece of removal.
