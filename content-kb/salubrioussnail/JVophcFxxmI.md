## Summary

The video defines a consistent deck as one that reliably does something, with its different lines of play close in power level, rather than doing the exact same thing every game. The creator cites his Glissa, Meria, and Rakdos decks as examples that run variations on a single theme. He argues consistency helps table politics (people remember reliable plans as scarier) and personal fun. For the 'how,' he covers rollout consistency—checking that the deck does something good in opening turns, best tested via heavy solo playtesting—and offers probability benchmarks: roughly 10 copies of an effect to see it in an opening hand over half the time; more ramp raises odds of hitting it by a target turn. He warns that 100 cards is more slots than people think, and surplus slots invite weak filler. His fix is categorizing the deck into Ramp, Draw, Removal, and Protection, questioning whether each card truly fits (e.g., is Gilded Lotus ramp?), and sizing categories—removal around 8–14, protection highly variable. He closes with situational/resilience consistency via tutors and 'toolbox' decks (Birthing Pod, Yisan, Sunforger), defending tutors as no worse than luck-based combo draws.

## Key Clips

- **[02:51]** One of the easier forms of consistency to nail is what I like to call "rollout consistency". This type of consistency basically asks the question, "do I do what I want to do on the first several turns of the game?"
- **[03:11]** The best way of doing this is solo playtesting a deck a whole bunch, and I generally playtest my decks quite a lot before spending any money on them.
- **[04:49]** A simple benchmark you can reference is that you need 10 of a card in a deck to have it show up in a given hand more than half the time.
- **[06:23]** A good first step toward reducing this sort of filler is to categorize your deck, and think about the categories. Four supporting categories I usually have are Ramp, Draw, Removal, and Protection.
- **[07:40]** For removal I'd recommend starting between 8 and 14, and adjust from there keeping in mind that slower decks and lower-velocity decks will usually want more removal than faster decks and higher-velocity decks.
- **[08:36]** If a card doesn't fit into one of your key categories, you should be asking yourself what its purpose is, and the answer should be simple and straightforward. If the answer involves scratching your head and vaguely gesturing at a couple other cards, that's a card that deserves a more robust evaluation.
- **[09:29]** The best way to allow a deck to have easy access to situational cards without destroying the deck's general consistency is tutors. A natural place to end up is often what's called a "toolbox" deck.
