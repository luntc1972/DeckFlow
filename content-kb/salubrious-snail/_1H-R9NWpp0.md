## Summary

The video argues that EDH has become the default casual format, and asks what primarily-commander players lose by never playing other formats. In 1v1 20-life formats, games are low and grindy, so every point of life is a resourceΓÇöteaching blocking discipline and life-total thresholds that EDH's higher life totals and one-or-two-shot wincons fail to instill. Limited and 60-card play also train players for low-resource, topdecking, attrition, and stax situations, where format-experienced players keep hunting for outs rather than conceding. For deckbuilding, non-EDH experience teaches players to question ramp rather than auto-include 8-10 staples regardless of fit; the speaker cites his own Malfegor and Francisco Malcolm decks that run only a few two-mana rocks plus Sol Ring. Singleton format also poorly teaches optimal card quantities, since 60-card formats let you flex counts up to four copies. The same critical eye applies to card draw and board wipesΓÇönominal EDH necessities that some decks don't need. Recommended formats: Pauper (cheap, vibrant all-commons), draft and cube for deckbuilders, and prereleases/sealed for newer players since rebuildable decks and variance forgive mistakes.

## Key Clips

- EDH has effectively become the casual way to play paper Magic. The days of 60 card kitchen table gaming are in the past, replaced by a diverging path for newer players: either play Arena and get your 1v1 fix, or meet up with three other people and play commander.
- Players of 20 health formats learn to internalize the idea that life total is a resource, and to know when to spend and when to save that resource, but commander players who have spent a lot of time dealing with bigger one-or-two-shot wincons won't internalize this lesson nearly as quickly.
- EDH's higher life totals also train players to mostly disregard the idea of blocking with useful creatures. Unlike in a 20 health format where blocking with a key creature to stay alive for an extra turn or two is a very common necessity, this isn't necessary nearly as often in EDH due to higher life totals and heftier wincons.
- When a game has involved some form of stax or heavy attrition and resources are dwindling, you can usually tell who has played other formats and who hasn't. A lot of primarily EDH players will be looking at the situation as a lost cause, whereas people who have done their time in the trenches of limited or 60 card formats will be more engaged and vigilant, looking for outs and bits of incremental advantage.
- In formats outside commander, ramp spells are viewed very criticallyΓÇöa player must ask themself 'why is this here?' and if that question doesn't have a clear and persuasive answer, the card will generally get cut. By contrast, many people building a commander deck will throw together their template-built EDH deck with the requisite count of ramp cards picked from among the available staples.
- In both my Malfegor deck and my Francisco Malcolm deck I don't want to spend the entirety of my turn 2 or 3 playing ramp spells, because I have more useful cards I want to be developing. Each deck runs only a low single digits quantity of two mana rocks so as not to draw more than one in the early stages of a game, plus a Sol Ring.
- A feature of singleton formats is that they restrict how many of a particular sort of card a player can run. When players have access to a greater number of a particularly appealing effect, if they've played EDH but not other formats, the instinct might be to load up on all available copies of that effect, regardless of how many would actually be optimal for the deck.
- As far as 60 card formats go, Pauper is one exceptionΓÇöthis all-commons format is cheap to get into and pretty vibrant, and its fast-paced but not overly complex gameplay makes it a great place to dive into 60 card Magic. For limited, draft and cube are skill-testing tasks; if those sound too intimidating, go to prereleases, which are sealed events.
