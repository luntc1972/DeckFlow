## Summary

This deck is Erinis, Gloom Stalker paired with the Street Urchin background, a Gruul (red-green) control deck. The command zone offers two separate engines: land recursion (ramping via Evolving Wilds-type lands, sac lands, harrow effects, and artifact lands) and a removal engine, where Erinis's deathtouch plus Street Urchin lets you pay 1 and sacrifice an artifact or creature to kill an opposing creature. The deck packs 19 token-generators plus 11 expendable artifacts/creatures as sac fodder (highlights: Scion of Calamity, Humble Defector, Treasure Vault), heavy draw and filtering, and protection spells to shield its command-zone reliance. Notably it runs almost no ramp outside the command zone (only Sol Ring), trading speed for a consistent curve. It wins by grinding out resources, controlling the board, then swinging with big creatures or buff-equipped Erinis. The reviewer judges it solidly mid-power and hard to upgrade: faster ramp, more protection, or stronger finishers each undercut consistency or its control identity, and a manabase upgrade costs ~$180. The deck's main issue is social, not mechanical—relentless creature removal can frustrate mid-power tables. Played sparingly with table buy-in, it enables a fun "archenemy" dynamic.

## Key Clips

- His deck's commander pairing is Erinis, Gloom Stalker with a background of Street Urchin. Street Urchin pairs with Erinis' deathtouch to turn into a removal engine, so the command zone effectively ends up offering two entirely separate things—land recursion, and removal.
- The removal engine in the command zone is pretty fearsome—at any point in time you can pay 1 and sacrifice an artifact or creature to kill an opposing creature. The deck packs 19 total cards that generate creature or artifact tokens, and 11 other artifacts and creatures that the deck is either indifferent about sacrificing, or actively wants to sacrifice.
- The only ramp card in the deck that isn't specifically supporting the command zone is Sol Ring. The deck could run dorks or rocks, but these options would eat up slots that could go to making sure the deck has lands in yard and stuff to sacrifice. The result is that the deck operates with a very consistent curve.
- Control decks can win games with less powerful finishers than midrange decks, simply because they generally have more time to work with and less on-board resistance to worry about. Once Gruul Control gets the game into a soft lock, hitting opponents three times each with an adventuring Erinis is a win plan I could absolutely see being viable.
- If you start replacing Harrow effects and treasure generators with ramp that lets you get out Erinis on turn 2, you'll get Erinis triggers earlier, but you'll on average have fewer lands in the graveyard and fewer tokens to sacrifice, negating some of that increase in speed.
- A lack of white or blue means that the deck will struggle to deal with certain types of removal, and this color pairing additionally limits the amount of stack interaction the deck is able to run to prevent opposing wins. This is the sort of deck that will really struggle to contain more explosive wins if it ever falls a bit behind, making decks that win with combos and noncreature means a tricky matchup.
- This deck's gameplan should be to outgrind, not outrun—generating resources and stabilizing the boardstate should be your focus, not rushing to find combo pieces. The most concrete upgrade starts with buying or proxying $180 worth of lands for a two color deck, itself a move that feels strange.
- As long as people know what the deck is capable of and to expect to get their creatures yeeted, it can play into a flexible "archenemy" dynamic, where the table tries to figure out how much resources to spend suppressing it. It's also a type of deck that should be played sparingly—pulling out that type of deck too often can lead to other people feeling like a side character in the four player game they're playing.
