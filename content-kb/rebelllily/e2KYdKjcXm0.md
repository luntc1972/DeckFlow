## Summary

The video argues players who lose often blame the table for not running enough removal, but the real key is using removal to advance your own win. Removal has four attributes: cost (cheaper is better), speed (instant preferred but pricier), range (single-target, multi, or sweep), and flexibility (variety of legal targets). In multiplayer it is tempo-negativeΓÇöa one-for-one trade only sets back you and one opponent while two others gain. Removal serves three roles: survival (reactive instant-speed answers to stop a win), unlocking (clearing defenses like Rule of Law, Propaganda, or Maze of Ith that block your plan), and suppression/deescalation (board wipes and engines valuing range and tempo). Recommended counts: ~12 removal as a baseline, fewer (12, unlock-focused) for aggressive decks, more (18, flexible, more wipes) for reactive decks. cEDH runs 4-8 single-target plus 8-10 counters. Build removal that fits your theme (Sundering Growth in tokens, Ancient Animus in counters decks) and adds flexibility (Bushwhack, Supreme Will, Final Showdown) or scaling engines (Aura Shards, Intrepid Hero). A joking fifth category: 'grifter removal'ΓÇötalking opponents into spending their own removal for you.

## Key Clips

- There's four important attributes of removal. First is cost ΓÇö the casting cost of removal; the cheaper it is, the better. Speed is the timing of when we can cast it; we always want things at instant speed, but usually the more reactive our removal is, the more it costs or the less it can target. Range is the amount of targets our removal deals with ΓÇö single target, multiple targets, or sweep everything. And lastly, flexibility: the variety of targets it can hit.
- Removal in multiplayer has a tricky issue of being tempo negative. You doom blade a creature. Now you and the owner of that creature go down one card and the resources spent to cast the creature and also the removal spell. The two other opponents are unaffected. The classic arcane denial argument.
- Removal has three main roles. They need to stop our opponents from winning or reaching the point of winning ΓÇö let's call this survival. They also need to help us push through our game plan ΓÇö let's call this unlocking. And lastly, they need to control the board for the sake of deescalation ΓÇö let's call this suppression.
- If we need to stop our opponents, speed matters most because it has to be reactive instant speed removal. Meanwhile, if our deck cares about a specific form of defense that stops our game plan altogether ΓÇö like a rule of law against a spells deck, a propaganda against a go wide deck, or amaze against a voltron deck ΓÇö this removal doesn't have to be at instant speed but could be more narrow. And deescalation cares more about range or tempo than anything else.
- From my precon statistics video, we know that Wizards builds with an average of 12 removal spells in a deck. A Wizards designer recommends to fill out this stack of 12 removal spells across three different types of removal. In cEDH, where interaction is at its maximum, we normally see a range of 4 to 8 single target removal with counter spells usually at 8 to 10.
- A deck that is aggressive in nature wants less removal, since most of our resources and time will be spent advancing our aggressive game plan. 12 removal spells should suffice, with a heavier lean towards removal that unlocks and less focus on board wipes. While a deck that is more reactive in nature will need more removal to suppress and control the board ΓÇö the deck will want 18 removal spells with more focus on flexibility and range and more board wipes to reset the table.
- Look for removal that fits your deck's theme. Cards like ancient animus for a +1/+1 counters deck to remove a creature while also giving counters to be proliferated, or Oblivion Ring-like effects for enchantments matters decks, or Sundering Growth in a tokens deck instead of disenchant. We can also look for removal that has flexibility beyond removing things ΓÇö spells like Bushwhack that can find you a land or remove a creature, or Final Showdown that can protect a creature at two mana, or wipe the board at instant speed for six.
- The perfect removal package isn't about following a rigid formula, but understanding your deck's needs and the meta you play in. The best removal packages help you execute your game plan while preventing opponents from executing theirs. So the next time an opponent complains that the table isn't running enough removal, just tell them, 'Have you tried winning instead?'
